# WT-026: Traditional Lime Plaster and Fresco Painting (传统石灰灰泥与湿壁画)

## 🏛️ 技术档案

- **技术名称**: Traditional Lime Plaster and Fresco / 传统石灰灰泥与湿壁画 (chuán tǒng shí huī huī ní yǔ shī bì huà)
- **技术领域**: Building Materials / Architectural Finishing / Wall Painting
- **活跃时期**: ~1500 BCE (Minoan Crete) → 20th century
- **鼎盛时期**: Renaissance Italy (14th-16th century), Baroque (17th century)
- **衰退时间**: Late 19th-20th century (Portland cement, gypsum board)
- **核心特征**: Breathable walls, carbonation curing, painting on wet plaster
- **关键人物**: Michelangelo (Sistine Chapel), Raphael, Leonardo da Vinci

---

## 📜 Brief History

**呼吸的墙与永恒的色彩。**
Ancient Minoans pioneered fresco (1500 BCE); Romans perfected lime plaster; Renaissance elevated fresco to high art; Portland cement (1824) and gypsum board (20th century) replaced breathable lime; conservation reveals lime's superiority for historic buildings.

## ⚰️ 衰退原因

### 1. **Portland Cement Superiority (for Structural Use)**
   - **Fast curing**: Portland cement sets in hours vs lime weeks
   - **High strength**: Compressive strength 10× lime
   - **Water resistance**: Portland cement less permeable
   - **Standardization**: Consistent industrial product
   - **Versatility**: Reinforced concrete enables modern architecture

### 2. **Gypsum Drywall (20th century)**
   - **Speed**: Pre-manufactured panels, instant installation
   - **Cost**: Mass production, cheap installation
   - **Consistency**: Uniform surface, predictable
   - **Fire resistance**: Gypsum naturally fire-resistant
   - **Labor reduction**: Skilled plasterers unnecessary

### 3. **Technical Limitations of Lime**
   - **Slow curing**: Carbonation takes weeks to months
   - **Weather sensitivity**: Rain during curing damages work
   - **Skill intensity**: Highly skilled plasterers needed
   - **Material variability**: Lime quality varies by source
   - **Application limitations**: Multiple thin coats required

### 4. **Industrialization and Standardization**
   - **Prefabrication**: Factory-made building components
   - **Modular construction**: Standardized dimensions
   - **Speed over quality**: Modern construction prioritizes speed
   - **Material availability**: Portland cement, gypsum widely available
   - **Code acceptance**: Building codes specify modern materials

### 5. **Knowledge and Skill Loss**
   - **Apprenticeship decline**: Fewer skilled plasterers trained
   - **Technical schools**: Teaching modern materials, not lime
   - **Written knowledge**: Much lime knowledge was oral, tacit
   - **Generation gap**: Older lime plasterers retired without successors
   - **Perceived obsolescence**: Lime seen as outdated technology

### 6. **Cost and Economic Factors**
   - **Labor costs**: Skilled lime plasterers expensive
   - **Time costs**: Slow curing = delayed completion
   - **Material costs**: Lime not significantly cheaper than alternatives
   - **Opportunity costs**: Faster completion = faster revenue
   - **Insurance and liability**: Modern materials have specified performance

## 🔬 Technical Deep Dive

### Lime Production and Chemistry

#### **Lime Burning (Calcination)**
```
Chemical process:
CaCO₃ (limestone) + heat → CaO (quicklime) + CO₂

Temperature: 900-1000°C (lower than cement's 1450°C)
Fuel: Wood, coal, gas (historically: wood, charcoal)
Kiln types: Traditional clamp kilns, shaft kilns, modern rotary kilns

Quicklime properties:
- Highly reactive with water (exothermic)
- Caustic (burns skin)
- Must be slaked before use
- Can be stored indefinitely (dry)

Historical lime burning:
- Rural production: Local limestone burned in field kilns
- Seasonal: Burned in dry seasons
- Regional variations: Different limestones produce different limes
- Knowledge transmission: Kiln-building, burning skills local
```

#### **Slaking (Hydration)**
```
Chemical process:
CaO (quicklime) + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ (calcium hydroxide/limewater) + heat

Exothermic reaction: Produces significant heat
Boiling possible: Enough heat to boil added water

Slaking methods:
1. Dry slaking: Add just enough water to produce powder
2. Wet slaking: Add excess water to produce lime putty
3. Hot slaking: Add hot water (produces finer lime)

Lime putty:
- Creamy consistency
- Ages improve quality (months to years)
- Stored wet (never dries out)
- "Fat lime": Well-slaked, workable

Historical practice:
- Lime pits: Large pits for slaking and storing
- Aging: Lime putty aged months/years
- "The older the better": Aged lime more workable, less shrinkage
```

#### **Carbonation (Curing)**
```
Chemical process:
Ca(OH)₂ + CO₂ → CaCO₃ (calcium carbonate) + H₂O

This reverses calcination:
- Limestone (CaCO₃) → Quicklime (CaO) → Lime putty (Ca(OH)₂) → Limestone (CaCO₃)
- Carbon dioxide from air recombines with lime
- Returns to original limestone composition

Curing characteristics:
- Slow process: Weeks to months
- Requires air circulation: CO₂ must reach plaster
- Moisture dependent: Too wet = no carbonation, too dry = no reaction
- Depth limited: Only surface carbonates (few millimeters)

Self-healing mechanism:
- Micro-cracks expose fresh lime
- Fresh lime carbonates, sealing crack
- Ongoing process over years/decades

Historical understanding:
- Not understood scientifically until 19th century
- Practical knowledge: "Lime needs to breathe"
- Traditional timing: Lime plaster given time to cure
```

### Lime Plaster Types

#### **Non-Hydraulic Lime (Traditional)**
```
Characteristics:
- Pure calcium lime (calcium oxide/hydroxide)
- Sets only by carbonation (air curing)
- Must be kept moist during carbonation
- Very slow curing (weeks to months)
- Soft, flexible, breathable

Production:
- Pure limestone (over 95% calcium carbonate)
- Burned at 900-1000°C
- Slaked to putty
- Aged before use

Uses:
- Interior plaster
- Fresco painting (only non-hydraulic suitable)
- Conservation of historic buildings
- Breathable finishes

Historical primary lime type
```

#### **Hydraulic Lime**
```
Characteristics:
- Contains clay impurities (silica, alumina)
- Sets by hydration + carbonation
- Faster curing than non-hydraulic
- Some water resistance (can set underwater)
- Harder, less flexible

Natural hydraulic lime (NHL):
- Classified by strength: NHL 2 (weak), NHL 3.5 (medium), NHL 5 (strong)
- Depends on clay content in original limestone
- Traditional: Used where faster set needed
- Exterior render, damp conditions

Historical use:
- Roman concrete: Hydraulic lime + pozzolana
- Medieval mortars: Naturally hydraulic limes
- Marine structures: Hydraulic lime for water resistance

Modern availability:
- Produced in France, Europe
- Increasingly used in conservation
- Compromise between traditional and modern
```

#### **Hybrid Lime-Cement (Historical Transition)**
```
Late 19th-early 20th century:
- Lime + Portland cement mixtures
- Attempted to combine lime's workability with cement's strength
- Common in early 20th century buildings

Problems:
- Cement dominates (harder, less permeable)
- Incompatible materials create problems
- Trapped moisture in historic buildings
- Conservation issues: Damage to soft historic fabric

Current conservation practice:
- Avoid cement in historic buildings
- Use pure lime for compatibility
- Remove cement renders from historic buildings
```

### Fresco Painting Technique

#### **Buon Fresco (True Fresco)**
```
Process:
1. Wall prepared with rough plaster (arriccio)
2. Smooth final coat (intonaco) applied daily
3. Artist paints on wet intonaco (only as much as can complete in day)
4. Pigments mixed with water, painted into wet plaster
5. Chemical reaction: Pigments bind into plaster as it carbonates
6. Once dry, painting is part of wall surface

Technical requirements:
- Only pigments that resist lime's alkalinity (earth pigments)
- No organic pigments (destroyed by lime)
- Quick execution: Must paint before plaster dries
- Planning: Cartoon (full-scale drawing) transferred to wall
- Skill: Years of apprenticeship

Advantages:
- Permanent: Painting integral to wall
- Durable: Lasts centuries (if wall intact)
- No fading: Pigments protected within plaster

Limitations:
- Color palette limited (lime-stable pigments only)
- No revisions possible (once dry, cannot change)
- Slow execution: Limited by drying time
- Weather constraints: Cannot paint in rain, freezing

Masterpieces:
- Michelangelo's Sistine Chapel (1508-1512)
- Raphael's Stanze (Vatican, 1508-1520)
- Giotto's Scrovegni Chapel (Padua, 1305)
```

#### **Fresco Secco (Dry Fresco)**
```
Process:
- Paint on dry lime plaster
- Binder added (usually casein, egg tempera)
- Pigments sit on surface, not integrated

Advantages:
- More colors possible (organic pigments stable)
- Can paint on already-dried plaster
- Can revise, repaint

Disadvantages:
- Less durable: Paint sits on surface
- Flaking, peeling over time
- Not true fresco

Historical use:
- Combined with buon fresco (details added secco)
- Small areas, touch-ups
- Less prestigious than buon fresco
```

### Application Techniques

#### **Three-Coat System (Traditional)**
```
1. Scratch coat (arriccio/trullisaggio):
   - Coarse aggregate (sand, gravel)
   - Thickest coat (10-20mm)
   - Scratch lines for key (next coat grip)
   - Purpose: Build thickness, provide base

2. Brown coat (arriccio/intonaco):
   - Medium sand
   - Intermediate thickness (5-10mm)
   - Smoothed surface
   - Purpose: Provide ground for final coat

3. Finish coat (intonaco):
   - Fine sand or marble dust
   - Thinnest coat (2-3mm)
   - Smooth, polished surface
   - For fresco: Painting surface (applied daily)

Historical practice:
- Each coat allowed to cure before next
- Moisture management: Dampen previous coat before next
- Curing time: Weeks to months total process
```

#### **Traditional Tools**
```
Application tools:
- Wooden floats: Trowel-shaped wooden tools
- Steel trowels: For smoothing (modern)
- Scratchers: Combed tools for keying
- Hawks: Small boards holding plaster
- Laths: Wooden strips backing plaster (lath and plaster)

Material preparation:
- Lime putty: Stored wet, aged
- Aggregates: Sand, marble dust, sieved and washed
- Hair: Animal hair (horse, goat) reinforcement (in scratch coat)
- Water: Clean, free of organics

Historical knowledge:
- Lime "fatness": Workability, stickiness
- Sand selection: Sharp, clean, well-graded
- Weather awareness: Protect from rain, sun, wind
- Seasonal work: Spring/autumn ideal (moderate temperature, humidity)
```

### Historical Examples

#### **Roman Lime Plasters (Pompeii, 1st century CE)**
   - **Sophistication**: Multi-colored frescoes, architectural illusions
   - **Durability**: Still vibrant after 2000 years
   - **Technique**: True fresco, multiple coats, polished surfaces
   - **Pigments**: Wide range (limited by lime alkalinity)
   - **Innovation**: Early use of lime for art and architecture

#### **Renaissance Frescoes (14th-16th century)**
   - **Sistine Chapel**: Michelangelo (1508-1512)
   - **Scrovegni Chapel**: Giotto (1305)
   - **Raphael Rooms**: Vatican (1508-1520)
   - **Significance**: Fresco as high art, major commissions
   - **Technique**: Buon fresco, planning cartoons, rapid execution

#### **Islamic Plasterwork (Alhambra, 14th century)**
   - **Decorative plaster**: Carved, molded stucco
   - **Geometric patterns: Complex Islamic designs
   - **Technique**: Carved into partially set plaster
   - **Durability**: Still intact after centuries
   - **Innovation**: Plaster as decorative medium, not just background

#### **Chinese Plaster (Traditional Architecture)**
   - **Lime in China**: Used since antiquity
   - **Tamp earth**: Rammed earth with lime stabilization
   - **Fine white lime**: Interior finishes
   - **Not fresco tradition**: Different painting approach (on dry surfaces)
   - **Regional variations**: Local techniques, materials

## 📖 Lessons from Lime Plaster and Fresco

### **关于技术与可持续性**

1. **Breathability as Design Principle**
   - Traditional: Buildings designed to breathe
   - Modern: Hermetically sealed buildings (sick building syndrome)
   - Lesson: Permeability can be feature, not bug
   - Application: Vapor-open building assemblies

2. **Slow Curing vs Fast Construction**
   - Lime: Weeks/months to cure properly
   - Modern: Hours/days (cement) or instant (drywall)
   - Trade-off: Speed vs longevity, health
   - Lesson: Not everything should be fast

3. **Appropriate Materials for Context**
   - Historic buildings need lime (built to breathe)
   - Modern materials damage historic fabric
   - Compatibility matters as much as performance
   - Lesson: Understanding original design philosophy essential

### **关于知识与技能**

4. **Tacit Knowledge and Loss**
   - Lime skills: Physical sensations, timing, judgment
   - Oral tradition: Much knowledge never written
   - Generational loss: Skills lost when masters die without successors
   - Lesson: Some knowledge only transmitted through practice

5. **Scientific Understanding vs Practical Knowledge**
   - Traditional: Practical knowledge without theory
   - Modern: Theory validates traditional practice
   - Carbonation chemistry: Explains what practitioners knew
   - Lesson: Science and craft can inform each other

6. **Recovering Lost Knowledge**
   - Lime techniques: Almost lost, now being rediscovered
   - Conservation: Researching traditional methods
   - Reverse engineering: Analyzing historic plaster
   - Lesson: Knowledge can be lost and recovered

### **关于美学与价值**

7. **Permanence in Art and Architecture**
   - Fresco: Painting becomes part of wall
   - Durability: Centuries of enjoyment
   - Modern: Ephemeral, replaceable
   - Lesson: Some things should be built to last

8. **Craftsmanship as Value**
   - Hand-applied: Human skill visible
   - Variation: Subtle, warm, alive
   - Modern: Uniform, sterile, machine-made
   - Lesson: Craftsmanship adds value beyond function

9. **Aging and Patina**
   - Lime: Develops patina, improves with age
   - Modern: Degrades, looks worn
   - Cultural value: Age as asset, not defect
   - Lesson: Materials that age gracefully

### **关于环境与健康**

10. **Indoor Environmental Quality**
    - Lime: Regulates humidity, inhibits mold
    - Modern: Traps moisture, promotes mold
    - Health: Breathing walls = healthy buildings
    - Lesson: Building materials affect occupant health

11. **Environmental Footprint**
    - Lime: Lower energy, CO2 reabsorption
    - Cement: 8% global CO2 emissions
    - Lesson: Traditional materials can be more sustainable

12. **Toxicity and Health**
    - Lime: High pH, antiseptic, non-toxic
    - Modern: VOCs, chemicals in building materials
    - Lesson: Natural materials often healthier
