# WT-065: Traditional Papermaking

## 🏛️ 技术名称和领域

**传统手工造纸** - 材料工艺/书写材料/文化载体

## 📜 简史

公元105年中国蔡伦改进造纸术，将植物纤维经过打浆、抄造、压榨、干燥制成纸张。8世纪传入阿拉伯世界，12世纪传入欧洲。传统手工造纸依赖植物纤维（如树皮、破布、棉麻），经过浸泡、打浆、抄造、压榨、干燥等工序。工业革命后机器造纸逐渐取代，但手工造纸在特定地区延续至20世纪末。当今作为文化遗产和奢侈品少量生产，大部分已被机制纸取代。

## ⚰️ 枯萎原因

机器造纸（19世纪初）生产速度快、成本低、质量均匀。手工造纸生产周期长、劳动密集、质量难以标准化。20世纪纸浆和造纸工业完全机械化、自动化，化学漂白技术普及。书写习惯改变（打字机、计算机）减少了对高档手工纸的需求。塑料和合成材料替代了部分纸张用途。手工造纸仅保留在艺术、文物修复、奢侈品等特殊领域。

## 💡 当代启发

造纸术作为中国古代四大发明之一，体现了**材料转化**的智慧：将植物纤维转化为平整书写载体。传统造纸的**低技术**特点（无需复杂设备、可本地生产、可生物降解）在可持续发展背景下重新被评估。手工造纸的**材料多样性**（不同植物纤维产生不同特性纸张）启发现代材料科学对**天然材料**的重新认识。作为信息载体的进化关键环节，造纸术的革命性意义提醒我们**基础材料技术**对文明发展的影响。当代手工纸复兴运动体现了对**物质性**和**工艺价值**的重新追求。

# Traditional Papermaking

## Technical Overview

Traditional papermaking represents one of humanity's most significant material innovations, enabling the recording and transmission of knowledge across cultures and centuries. For nearly two millennia, hand papermaking techniques evolved and adapted to local materials and needs, creating diverse paper traditions that served as the foundation for literate civilization.

### Fundamental Principles

**Fiber network formation**:
- **Cellulose fibers**: Plant fibers provide structural matrix
- **Hydrogen bonding**: Hydroxyl groups on cellulose form hydrogen bonds when dried
- **Mat formation**: Fibers interlock to create continuous sheet
- **Drying**: Water removal activates bonding, creates stable sheet

**Material requirements**:
- **Fiber source**: Plant fibers with sufficient length and strength
- **Purity**: Minimal non-fibrous material
- **Processability**: Fibers must separate and recombine in network
- **Availability**: Sustainable supply for production

### Raw Materials

**Fiber sources varied by region**:

**East Asian traditions**:
- **Paper mulberry (Broussonetia papyrifera)**: Most common in China, Korea, Japan
- **Bamboo**: Used in China, provides long fibers
- **Hemp**: Important early material, strong fibers
- **Rice straw**: Common, shorter fibers
- **Cotton linters**: Later period, high quality

**Islamic world**:
- **Flax (Linum usitatissimum)**: Linen rags, excellent quality
- **Hemp**: Rags from rope and textiles
- **Cotton**: Increasingly important with cotton cultivation
- **Mixed fibers**: Whatever rags available

**European traditions**:
- **Cotton and linen rags**: Primary material for centuries
- **Hemp**: Rags from ropes and sails
- **Esparto grass**: Iberian specialty, 19th century
- **Wood pulp**: Late 19th century transition to industrial

**Regional specializations**:
- **Japan**: Mulberry (kozo), mitsumata, gampi (three traditional fibers)
- **Nepal**: Daphne bush fibers (lokta)
- **India**: Cotton and jute fibers
- **Burma**: Mulberry and bamboo

### Manufacturing Process

**Traditional process (generalizable across cultures)**:

**1. Fiber preparation**:
- **Collection**: Gathering raw materials
- **Sorting**: Removing impurities
- **Cutting**: Chopping to appropriate length
- **Retting**: (some fibers) Controlled bacterial/fungal decay to separate fibers
- **Washing**: Cleaning fibers

**2. Beating/pulping**:
- **Traditional**: Wooden mallets or hammers to separate fibers
- **Water-powered**: Stamp mills (medieval Europe) to beat fibers
- **Hollander beater** (17th century Netherlands): Improved beater
- **Goal**: Separate individual fibers while preserving length
- **Duration**: Hours to days depending on material and beater type

**3. Sheet formation**:
- **Vat preparation**: Fiber suspended in water
- **Mold screening**: Wire or bamboo screen dipped into vat
- **Shake**: Distribute fibers evenly on screen
- **Couching**: Transfer wet sheet to felt (stacking alternating sheets)
- **Couching**: (pronounced 'cooching') skilled operation

**4. Pressing**:
- **Initial**: Hand pressing to remove water
- **Screw press**: Stack of sheets pressed under pressure
- **Gradation**: Progressive pressure to avoid damaging sheets
- **Duration**: Hours to days depending on thickness

**5. Drying**:
- **Air drying**: Sheets hung on ropes or dried on boards
- **Press drying**: Continued pressing during drying
- **Sizing**: (often) Surface treatment with sizing (starch, gelatin)
- **Finishing**: Calendering for smoothness, burnishing

**6. Sizing** (often, but not always):
- **Purpose**: Improve strength, reduce ink absorbency
- **Materials**: Starch, gelatin, or glue
- **Application**: Dipping or brushing onto dried sheets
- **Redrying**: Second drying after sizing

### Regional Variations

**Chinese tradition**:
- **Flexible mold**: Bamboo strips with silk screen
- **Drying**: Brushed on heated wall (early) or board
- **Sizing**: Often starch-based
- **Cultural integration**: Calligraphy and brushwork influenced paper characteristics

**Japanese tradition** (washi):
- **Cold water**: Fibers processed in cold water
- **Neri**: Tororo-ao (hibiscus) mucilage as formation aid
- **Drying**: Brushed onto wooden boards, air dried
- **No sizing**: Often unsized, absorbent for brushwork
- **Applications**: Particularly suited for calligraphy and woodblock printing

**Islamic world**:
- **Trip hammer**: Water-powered fulling mills adapted for beating
- **Sizing**: Starch sizing common
- **Finishing**: Burnished with stones
- **Quality**: High quality paper for Quran and manuscripts

**European tradition**:
- **Wire mesh**: Brass or copper wire screens
- **Felt couching**: Wool felts for water absorption
- **Sizing**: Gelatin (animal glue) sizing
- **Watermarking**: Wire designs in mold for identification
- **Finishing**: Calendering for smoothness

### Technical Characteristics

**Physical properties**:
- **Thickness**: Varies from tissue to heavy card
- **Density**: Affects strength and opacity
- **Formation quality**: Uniformity of fiber distribution
- **Surface texture**: Rough to smooth depending on drying and finishing

**Optical properties**:
- **Opacity**: Transparency varies with formation and density
- **Color**: Natural off-white to bleached white
- **Surface**: Mat to glossy depending on finish

**Mechanical properties**:
- **Tensile strength**: Affected by fiber length and bonding
- **Folding endurance**: Critical for books and documents
- **Tear resistance**: Important for durability
- **Dimensional stability**: Responds to humidity changes

**Absorption properties**:
- **Ink absorbency**: Varies with sizing and material
- **Water absorbency**: Influenced by sizing
- **Chemical reactivity**: Affected by pH, additives

### Quality Factors

**Material quality**:
- **Fiber length**: Longer fibers generally stronger
- **Fiber purity**: Minimal non-fibrous material
- **Fiber preparation**: Proper beating critical

**Process quality**:
- **Formation**: Uniform fiber distribution
- **Pressing**: Proper removal of water without damage
- **Drying**: Appropriate temperature and humidity
- **Sizing**: Proper application and penetration

**Skill requirements**:
- **Vatman**: Forming sheets requires skill and experience
- **Coucher**: Transferring sheets without damage
- **Press operator**: Judging appropriate pressure
- **Finishing**: Calendering and burnishing skill

## Brief History

### Chinese Invention and Early Development (1st century CE - 8th century)

**Traditional attribution**:
- **Cai Lun (蔡伦)**: Court official who improved papermaking, 105 CE
- **Earlier evidence**: Archaeological finds suggest paper existed earlier
- **Innovation**: Standardized process using readily available materials
- **Report to Emperor**: Brought paper to Emperor He's attention

**Early materials**:
- **Mulberry bark**: Primary fiber source
- **Hemp**: Important early material
- **Rags**: Used occasionally
- **Fishnets**: Recycled worn fishing nets

**Early uses**:
- **Wrapping**: Initially used for wrapping and padding
- **Writing**: Gradually adopted for writing
- **Replacement**: Replaced bamboo slips and silk for writing
- **Bureaucracy**: Government record-keeping

**Spread in East Asia**:
- **Korea**: 4th century, possibly earlier
- **Japan**: 6th century, along with Buddhism
- **Vietnam**: Along with Chinese cultural influence
- **Regional adaptations**: Different local materials and techniques

### Islamic World Development (8th century - 13th century)

**Transmission**:
- **Battle of Talas (751)**: Chinese papermakers captured by Abbasids
- **Samarkand**: Early Islamic papermaking center
- **Baghdad**: Major center by 8th century
- **Spread**: Throughout Islamic world by 10th century

**Innovations**:
- **Trip hammer**: Adapted from fulling mills for beating fibers
- **Sizing**: Starch sizing improved
- **Calligraphy**: Paper suited to Arabic calligraphic traditions
- **Book production**: Enabled book copying culture

**Materials**:
- **Flax and hemp rags**: Primary material
- **Cotton**: As cotton cultivation spread
- **Esparto**: Later, in Spain
- **Recycling**: Rags from textiles continuously recycled

**Quality and reputation**:
- **High quality**: Islamic paper highly regarded
- **Export**: Paper exported to Europe
- **Spanish paper**: Particularly fine quality, exported to Italy and elsewhere
- **Watermarks**: Introduced identification marks

### European Adaptation and Development (12th century - 18th century)

**Introduction to Europe**:
- **Spain**: Islamic influence brought papermaking
- **Italy**: Papermaking by 12th century, Fabriano famous
- **France**: Developed by 13th century
- **Germany**: Papermaking spread in 14th century

**Material transition**:
- **Rag-based**: Cotton and linen rags primary material
- **Rag collection**: Major industry, rags valuable commodity
- **Rag shortage**: Chronic problem, limited production
- **Recycling**: Rags used repeatedly until exhausted

**Technical developments**:
- **Water-powered mills**: Trip hammers for beating
- **Wire mesh screens**: Replaced reed screens
- **Sizing**: Gelatin (animal glue) sizing
- **Watermarks**: Identification and decoration
- **Improvements**: Better quality, larger sheets

**Guild regulation**:
- **Trade secrets**: Techniques guarded
- **Quality standards**: Guilds maintained standards
- **Training**: Apprenticeship systems
- **Geographic specialization**: Different regions specialized in different papers

**Printing revolution impact**:
- **Gutenberg (1450s)**: Printing created enormous demand for paper
- **Paper supply challenge**: Struggle to meet demand
- **Rag crisis**: Rag shortage became acute
- **Price pressures**: High prices encouraged innovation

### Industrial Revolution Transition (18th century - 19th century)

**Rag shortage crisis**:
- **Growing demand**: From printing and literacy
- **Limited supply**: Rag collection couldn't keep pace
- **High prices**: Paper expensive
- **Search for alternatives**: Explored non-rag materials

**Wood pulp development**:
- **Experiments**: Various wood pulping methods tested
- **Groundwood**: Mechanical pulping (1840s)
- **Chemical pulping**: Soda process (1851), sulfite process (1867)
- **Kraft process**: Sulfate process (1870s)

**Machine development**:
- **Nicholas-Louis Robert**: First paper machine (1799)
- **Fourdrinier brothers**: Improved and commercialized (1803)
- **Continuous production**: Machine made continuous paper
- **Scale**: Dramatic increase in production capacity

**Transition dynamics**:
- **Hand vs. machine**: Coexistence for decades
- **Quality issues**: Early machine paper inferior
- **Cost advantages**: Machine paper much cheaper
- **Gradual replacement**: Hand papermaking increasingly marginal

### 20th Century Decline of Hand Papermaking

**Competition from machine production**:
- **Cost**: Machine paper vastly cheaper
- **Speed**: Machine production orders of magnitude faster
- **Consistency**: Machine paper uniform quality
- **Scale**: Industrial scale production

**Changing demand**:
- **Writing habits**: Typewriter, then computer changed paper needs
- **Printing**: Offset printing required different paper characteristics
- **Packaging**: Cardboard and packaging materials mechanized
- **Specialized papers**: Machine production of specialized papers

**Niche persistence**:
- **Art papers**: Handmade papers for artists
- **Book arts**: Fine printing and bookbinding
- **Conservation**: Archival and restoration papers
- **Specialty**: Security papers, certain currency papers
- **Craft tradition**: Maintenance of hand papermaking traditions

**Traditional preservation efforts**:
- **Japan**: Washi traditions maintained
- **Europe**: Some hand papermills survived as tourist attractions/craft
- **Revival movements**: Interest in traditional craft techniques
- **UNESCO recognition**: Some traditions recognized as intangible cultural heritage

## Why It Withered

### Fundamental Economic Disadvantages

**Production speed**:
- **Hand**: Limited production, skilled labor intensive
- **Machine**: Continuous production, minimal labor per sheet
- **Scale difference**: Machines produce in hours what hand production takes weeks

**Labor costs**:
- **Hand**: Requires skilled, experienced workers
- **Machine**: Unskilled or semiskilled labor
- **Training**: Hand papermaking requires years of apprenticeship
- **Labor competition**: Industrial labor cheaper per unit output

**Material costs**:
- **Hand**: Rag collection expensive, limited supply
- **Machine**: Wood pulp cheap and abundant
- **Scale**: Wood pulp production at enormous scale
- **Availability**: Wood resources much larger than rag supply

**Standardization**:
- **Hand**: Quality varies by skill, materials, conditions
- **Machine**: Uniform, consistent quality
- **Customer preference**: Predictability valued
- **Industrial requirements**: Standard materials for industrial processes

### Technological Lock-out and Industrial Development

**Machine integration**:
- **Printing**: Offset printing requires consistent paper
- **Typewriters**: Machine paper optimal
- **Computing printers**: Very specific requirements
- **Industrial systems**: Designed around machine paper characteristics

**Chemical processing**:
- **Sizing**: Machine sizing cheaper, more consistent
- **Coatings**: Machine-applied coatings
- **Additives**: Chemical additives for specific properties
- **Bleaching**: Chemical bleaching achieves consistent whiteness

**Quality improvements**:
- **Machine paper**: Improved dramatically over 19th-20th centuries
- **Specialty papers**: Machines produce very high quality paper
- **Consistency**: Uniform quality from sheet to sheet
- **Customization**: Machines produce paper for specific needs

### Changing Usage Patterns and Requirements

**Writing technology changes**:
- **Fountain pens**: Required different paper than quills
- **Typewriters**: Required smooth, strong paper
- **Computer printers**: Require very specific paper characteristics
- **Digital communication**: Reduces paper use

**Cultural shifts**:
- **Casualness**: Less formal writing traditions
- **Disposable**: Paper used more casually, not precious commodity
- **Volume**: Much higher paper consumption
- **Cost sensitivity**: Unit cost more important than longevity

**Competition from alternatives**:
- **Digital media**: Reduces paper demand for some applications
- **Plastic**: Packaging alternatives
- **Electronic displays**: Replace some paper uses
- **Communication**: Digital alternatives to written correspondence

### Social and Cultural Factors

**Craft skill disappearance**:
- **Apprenticeship decline**: Less opportunity to learn traditional skills
- **Economic pressure**: Hand papermaking not economically viable
- **Training**: Fewer training opportunities
- **Knowledge transmission**: Traditional knowledge threatened

**Cultural revaluation**:
- **Heritage recognition**: Traditional craft valued as cultural heritage
- **Artisanal revival**: Interest in handmade, traditional products
- **Luxury market**: High-end market for artisanal products
- **Educational value**: Craft techniques taught as heritage skills

But these preservation efforts insufficient to maintain hand papermaking as broadly practiced technology.
