# WT-025: Traditional Hand Papermaking (传统手工造纸)

## 🏛️ 技术档案

- **技术名称**: Traditional Hand Papermaking / 传统手工造纸 (chuán tǒng shǒu gōng zào zhǐ)
- **技术领域**: Craft Production / Paper Technology / Material Science
- **活跃时期**: ~105 CE (China) → 19th century
- **鼎盛时期**: Song Dynasty China (960-1279), European handmade era (15th-18th century)
- **衰退时间**: 19th century (Fourdrinier machine 1803)
- **核心特征**: Sheet formation using frame and screen, plant fiber pulp
- **关键人物**: Cai Lun (105 CE), Fourdrinier brothers (1803)

---

## 📜 Brief History

**两千年手抄纸。**
Chinese invented paper (105 CE); spread to Islamic world (8th century), Europe (12th century); hand papermaking dominated 1800+ years; industrial revolution introduced continuous paper machine (1803); hand production survived for specialty papers, art.

---

## ⚰️ 衰退原因

### 1. **Industrial Mechanization (19th century)**
   - **Fourdrinier machine (1803)**: Continuous paper production
   - **Speed difference**: Hand: 500 sheets/day vs Machine: 1000+ feet/minute
   - **Cost reduction**: Machine paper 1/10th price of handmade
   - **Scale**: Industrial production: tons vs pounds daily
   - **Standardization**: Uniform sheet size, quality

### 2. **Economic Pressure and Market Demand**
   - **Literacy expansion**: Rising demand for books, newspapers
   - **Printing revolution**: Mass printing required cheap paper
   - **Administrative needs**: Bureaucracy, record-keeping
   - **Packaging growth**: Industrial goods needed wrapping
   - **Handmade couldn't scale**: Limited by human labor

### 3. **Raw Material Changes**
   - **Rag scarcity**: Cotton rags insufficient for industrial demand
   - **Wood pulp invention (1840s)**: Cheap, abundant fiber source
   - **Chemical processing**: Kraft process (1880s) enabled wood pulp
   - **Supply constraints**: Rag collection couldn't match machine capacity
   - **Material cost**: Wood pulp cheaper than cotton rags

### 4. **Quality vs Quantity Trade-off**
   - **Handmade advantages**: Durability, texture, archival quality
   - **Market preference**: Cheap, plentiful paper over durable quality
   - **Short-term thinking**: Most documents temporary, not for centuries
   - **Acid paper problem**: Wood pulp paper acidic, deteriorates
   - **Lost quality**: "Slow paper" vs "fast paper"

### 5. **Craft Skills and Training**
   - **Long apprenticeship**: 7+ years to master vatman, coucher, layer
   - **Physical demands**: Strenuous labor in wet conditions
   - **Specialized knowledge**: Beating, cooking, washing, forming
   - **Declining interest**: Industrial jobs more attractive
   - **Skill loss**: Each generation fewer master papermakers

### 6. **Technological Lock-In**
   - **Equipment investment**: Mills expensive, specialized
   - **Institutional inertia**: Hand mills couldn't convert to machine
   - **Market shift**: Customers switched to machine paper
   - **Supply chain**: Rag collection system disintegrated
   - **Obsolescence**: Handmade became luxury niche, not mainstream

## 💡 Contemporary Insights

### 1. **Archival Quality and Longevity**
   - **Handmade paper**: Lasts 500-1000+ years (pH neutral, strong fibers)
   - **Machine paper (1850-1980)**: Acidic, brittle within 50-100 years
   - **Library crisis**: 19th-20th century books crumbling
   - **Acid-free movement**: Return to pH-neutral paper
   - **Lesson**: Cheap production has hidden long-term costs

### 2. **Artisanal Craft and Value**
   - **Unique qualities**: Deckle edges, texture, watermark
   - **Artist papers**: Watercolor, printmaking demand handmade
   - **Luxury market**: Stationery, fine books, certificates
   - **Craft appreciation**: "Slow movement" values labor-intensive production
   - **Economic model**: High value, low volume vs low value, high volume

### 3. **Sustainable and Local Production**
   - **Local fibers**: Regional plants (kozo, gampi, cotton, linen)
   - **Recycled materials**: Rag paper using textile waste
   - **Low energy**: Human-powered, no chemicals (traditional)
   - **Biodegradable**: Natural materials, no pollution
   - **Appropriate technology**: Small-scale, community-based

### 4. **Material Science and Fiber Understanding**
   - **Fiber length**: Long fibers (cotton, linen) = strong paper
   - **Formation**: Sheet formation affects paper properties
   - **Sizing**: Gelatin, starch sizing controls ink absorption
   - **Couching**: Transfer technique affects surface quality
   - **Pressing and drying**: Affects flatness, strength

### 5. **Cultural Heritage Preservation**
   - **Living traditions**: Japanese washi, Chinese xuan paper
   - **Intangible heritage**: UNESCO recognition (washi 2014)
   - **Museum collections**: Historical papers studied, preserved
   - **Craft transmission**: Masters training apprentices
   - **Tourism and education**: Papermaking demonstrations

### 6. **Environmental Concerns**
   - **Forestry impact**: Industrial papermaking = deforestation
   - **Water pollution**: Chemical pulping, bleaching
   - **Energy consumption**: Massive industrial energy use
   - **Waste**: Overproduction, paper waste
   - **Alternative models**: Handmade as sustainable option

### 7. **Digital Parallels and Questions**
   - **Ephemeral vs permanent**: Digital files vs paper archives
   - **Format obsolescence**: Digital formats unreadable, paper always readable
   - **Production costs**: Near-zero digital distribution vs physical media
   - **Tactile experience**: Screen vs paper reading, writing
   - **Value in material**: Digital lacks materiality

### 8. **Craft Revival Movements**
   - **Book arts**: Letterpress, hand bookbinding
   - **Printmaking**: Etching, lithography demand quality paper
   - **Artist books**: Handmade papers integral
   - **Workshops**: Community papermaking classes
   - **Maker movement**: DIY papermaking from garden plants

## 🔬 Technical Deep Dive

### Traditional Papermaking Process

#### **1. Fiber Preparation**
```
Raw materials:
- Plant fibers: Kozo (mulberry), gampi, hemp, cotton, linen rags
- Rags (Europe/America): Old cotton/linen clothing, textiles
- Bark (Japan): Inner bark of woody plants

Cleaning and sorting:
- Remove contaminants: Buttons, zippers, dirt
- Sort by fiber type and color
- Cut into small pieces (1-2 inches)

Historical rag collection:
- "Rag and bone men": Collected rags door-to-door
- Import: Britain imported rags from colonies
- Value: Rags had economic value, not waste
- Scarcity: Limited supply constrained production
```

#### **2. Retting and Breaking Down**
```
Retting (controlled rotting):
- Soak fibers in water (days to weeks)
- Bacteria break down pectin (binds fibers)
- Natural process, weather-dependent
- Traditional: River retting (flowing water)

Alternative methods:
- Chemical cooking: Lye, soda ash (alkaline)
- Mechanical beating: Stampers (water-powered or animal-powered)
- Holland beater (17th century): Improved mechanical beating

Result: Individual fibers separated, ready for pulping
Time: 1-2 weeks (retting) or hours (mechanical beating)
```

#### **3. Beating and Pulping**
```
Stamping mills (water-powered):
- Wooden stamps with metal heads
- Pound fibers in water
- Fibrillate fibers (split ends)
- Increase surface area for bonding

Holland beater (innovation, 17th century):
- Rollers with metal bars
- Circulates pulp through bedplate
- More controlled fibrillation
- Produces stronger paper

Consistency assessment:
- "Rotten" properly: Fibers separate, hydrated
- Over-beating: Short fibers, weak paper
- Under-beating: Fibers clump, uneven sheet

Slurry: Pulp mixed with water (99% water, 1% fiber)
```

#### **4. Sheet Formation (Vatman's Work)**
```
Mold and deckle:
- Mold: Wire screen (metal or reed) on wooden frame
- Deckle: Removable wooden frame (creates deckle edge)
- Wire design: Watermark created by bent wire

Forming the sheet:
1. Vatman agitates vat (keeps fibers suspended)
2. Dip mold and deckle into pulp slurry
3. Lift out, allowing water to drain
4. Shake mold (parallel and perpendicular motion)
5. Fibers interlock, mat forms
6. Water drains through screen

Critical skills:
- Shake motion: Determines fiber orientation, strength
- Pulp consistency: Too thick = uneven, too thin = weak
- Speed: Must work quickly before fibers settle

Sheet size: Limited by arm strength (largest ~30 × 40 inches)
```

#### **5. Couching (Coucher's Work)**
```
Transfer process:
1. Coucher receives formed sheet from vatman
2. Post (stack) of felts alternated with paper sheets
3. Couch (transfer) sheet onto felt
4. Felts: Woolen blankets (woven or felted)
5. Water transfers from paper to felt

Coucher skills:
- Quick, precise transfer
- No tearing or wrinkling
- Proper alignment
- Maintaining rhythm (keep up with vatman)

Teamwork:
- Vatman + coucher work in coordinated rhythm
- Third worker (layer) builds post
- Efficient team: 500+ sheets per day
```

#### **6. Pressing**
```
Screw press (traditional):
- Post placed in wooden press
- Screw tightened (lever or wheel)
- Pressure removes water
- Compresses sheets

Pressing sequence:
1. Light press: Remove excess water
2. Tighten gradually: Avoid damaging sheets
3. Final pressure: Compress to ~20% original thickness

Time: Several hours to overnight

Alternative: Brake press (toggle mechanism)
Faster, less labor-intensive
```

#### **7. Drying**
```
Loft drying:
- Sheets hung on ropes (horses)
- Separated to prevent sticking
- Natural drying (days)
- Controlled environment (temperature, humidity)

Scaffold drying:
- Sheets placed on wooden frames (scaffolds)
- Dried flat
- Better for book paper (flatter)

Seasonal variation:
- Winter: Drying slow (heated rooms)
- Summer: Drying fast (risk of cockling)
- Humidity: Affects drying rate

Result: Dry paper sheets, ready for sizing
```

#### **8. Sizing**
```
Purpose: Control ink absorption, strengthen paper

Materials:
- Gelatin: Animal protein (hide, hoofs)
- Starch: Wheat, rice, potato
- Alum: Alters pH, preserves

Process (dip sizing):
1. Prepare warm size solution
2. Dip sheets individually
3. Hang to dry again
4. Resize if needed

Alternative (tub sizing):
- Sheets soaked in size bath
- Used for archival, art papers

Effects:
- Reduces bleeding (ink spreads)
- Improves strength
- Creates writable surface
- Affects archival quality (gelatin = archival)

Modern sizing:
- Engine sizing: Size added to pulp (machine process)
- Synthetic sizes: Chemical additives
```

#### **9. Finishing**
```
Calendaring (optional):
- Sheets pressed between polished rollers
- Smooths surface
- Compresses slightly
- Creates glazed or satin finish

Cutting:
- Sheets cut to size (if needed)
- Deckle edges retained (handmade characteristic)
- trimming for uniformity

Quality control:
- Inspection for defects
- Sorting by quality
- Grading: Royal, Super Royal, Imperial (sizes)

Packaging:
- Reams (500 sheets)
- Wrapped in paper
- Labeled with size, quality

Finished product: Ready for writing, printing, bookbinding
```

### Regional Variations

#### **Chinese Papermaking (Xuan Paper)**
```
Materials:
- Paper mulberry (kozo)
- Rice straw
- Blue sandalwood bark

Process:
- Fermentation pits (controlled rotting)
- Long fiber preparation
- Extremely thin, strong sheets
- Traditional brush painting surface

Characteristics:
- Absorbent (ink spreads)
- Strong (wet strength)
- Archival (centuries-old paintings survive)
- Crisp sound when handled

Use: Calligraphy, brush painting
```

#### **Japanese Washi Paper**
```
Fibers:
- Kozo (paper mulberry)
- Gampi (shrub, inner bark)
- Mitsumata (shrub, branches)

Process innovations:
- Long, cold fermentation (months)
- Tororo-aoi (hibiscus root) as formation aid
- Hand-beating with wooden mallets
- Winter production (cold water = cleaner fibers)

Characteristics:
- Very strong (long fibers)
- Warm, natural feel
- Translucent (thin sheets)
- Dimensionally stable

Uses:
- Shoji screens (translucent, strong)
- Printmaking (absorbent, strong wet)
- Conservation (archival quality)
- Clothing (traditional paper clothing)
- Architecture (paper walls, doors)

UNESCO recognition: 2014 (intangible cultural heritage)
```

#### **European Rag Paper**
```
Materials:
- Cotton and linen rags
- Hemp (lower quality)
- Esparto grass (19th century, Spain)

Process:
- Rag sorting (critical for quality)
- Dutch beating (Holland beater)
- Gelatin sizing
- Laid and chain lines (wire screen pattern)

Characteristics:
- Off-white (natural linen/cotton color)
- Strong, durable
- Holds ink well (sized surface)
- Laid lines visible (wire pattern)

Watermark innovation (13th century Italy):
- Wire design sewn to mold
- Logo, symbol, or text
- Identification (mill, papermaker)
- Anti-counterfeiting (currency, documents)

Uses:
- Books (printing)
- Writing (correspondence)
- Art (drawing, watercolor)
- Documents (legal, official)
- Currency (banknotes)
```

### Historical Examples

#### **Arab Papermaking (8th-15th century)**
   - **Spread**: From China to Samarkand, Baghdad, Damascus, Cairo
   - **Innovation**: Improved beating, tripartite mold
   - **Materials**: Linen, flax rags (no mulberry available)
   - **Quality**: Very fine, white, strong
   - **Transmission**: Brought papermaking to Europe via Spain

#### **Fabriano (Italy, 13th-14th century)**
   - **Innovations**: Watermark, gelatin sizing, improved wire molds
   - **Quality**: Finest European paper
   - **Trade**: Exported throughout Europe
   - **Significance**: Paper quality enabled Renaissance printing
   - **Legacy**: Still produces fine art paper today

#### **Japanese Papermaking (Traditional)**
   - **Locations**: Echizen (Fukui), Mino (Gifu), Tosa (Kochi)
   - **Specialization**: Each region known for specific papers
   - **Craft families**: Traditional knowledge passed through generations
   - **Living tradition**: Still producing today (mechanically and handmade)
   - **UNESCO**: Recognition as intangible cultural heritage

#### **English Hand Papermills (18th century)**
   - **Whatman paper**: James Whatman (senior and junior)
   - **Innovation**: Wove paper (no laid lines), sizing improvements
   - **Quality**: Finest drawing and watercolor paper
   - **Customers**: J.M.W. Turner, John Sell Cotman (artists)
   - **Survival**: Still producing (machine-made) fine art paper

## 🎭 Cultural Phenomena

### **"Rag and Bone Men" - Material Economy**
   - **Rag collection**: Urban waste collection system
   - **Economic value**: Rags had worth, not trash
   - **Global trade**: International rag trade
   - **Scarcity constraint**: Limited paper production
   - **Recycling precedent**: Early example of circular economy

### **Watermarks as Identity and Security**
   - **Mill identification**: Watermark showed papermaker
   - **Quality guarantee**: Reputable mill's watermark
   - **Currency security**: Complex watermarks prevented counterfeiting
   - **Artisan pride**: Craftsmanship visible in watermark quality
   - **Historical research**: Watermarks help date documents

### **Papermaking Guilds and Secrets**
   - **Trade secrets**: Techniques protected, not written down
   - **Apprenticeship**: Long training (7+ years)
   - **Guild control**: Regulated who could make paper
   - **Regional specialization**: Different areas known for specific papers
   - **Family traditions**: Knowledge passed within families

### **"Slow Paper" vs "Fast Paper"**
   - **Handmade**: "Slow paper" (patient production, durable)
   - **Machine**: "Slow paper" derogatory term for handmade (historical)
   - **Modern reversal**: Handmade "slow paper" now valued
   - **Philosophical**: Time investment creates value
   - **Environmental**: Fast production = hidden costs

### **Papermaking as Cultural Heritage**

## 📖 Lessons from Hand Papermaking

### **关于技术与价值**

1. **Speed vs Quality Trade-off**
   - Machine: Fast, cheap, ephemeral
   - Handmade: Slow, expensive, durable
   - Market chose fast (most uses don't need centuries-long life)
   - Lesson: Not everything needs to last forever (but some things should)

2. **Appropriate Scale and Technology**
   - Handmade: Human-scale, community-based
   - Machine: Industrial, centralized
   - Different appropriate for different uses
   - Lesson: Technology should match purpose, not maximize one metric

3. **Hidden Costs of Cheap Production**
   - Acid paper: Libraries spend millions deacidifying brittle books
   - Environmental: Pollution, deforestation from industrial paper
   - Short-term savings = long-term costs
   - Lesson: Cheap production has hidden externalities

### **关于材料与可持续性**

4. **Material Knowledge and Quality**
   - Rag paper: Cotton/linen fibers long, strong
   - Wood pulp: Short fibers, acidic chemicals
   - Material choice determines product lifespan
   - Lesson: Understanding materials essential for quality

5. **Local Resources vs Global Supply Chains**
   - Handmade: Local fibers, local production
   - Machine: Global forestry, chemical supply chains
   - Resilience: Local systems more resilient
   - Lesson: Bioregional production reduces vulnerability

6. **Recycling and Circular Economy**
   - Rag paper: Early recycling model (textiles to paper)
   - Value in waste: Rags had economic value
   - Modern: Industrial paper recycling (but downcycling)
   - Lesson: True circular economy maintains material quality

### **关于知识与传承**

7. **Tacit Knowledge and Apprenticeship**
   - Papermaking skills: Physical sensations, timing
   - Difficult to codify: Must learn by doing
   - Apprentice system: Long training, direct transmission
   - Lesson: Some knowledge only transmitted through practice

8. **Loss and Recovery of Craft**
   - 19th century: Almost lost (industrialization)
   - 20th century: Rediscovery by artists, conservators
   - 21st century: Recognition of value
   - Lesson: Craft knowledge can be lost and rediscovered

9. **Standardization vs Diversity**
   - Machine: Uniform, standardized
   - Handmade: Diverse, regional variations
   - Ecological and cultural value in diversity
   - Lesson: Standardization trades efficiency for diversity

### **关于艺术与工艺**

10. **Materiality in Digital Age**
    - Digital: Dematerialized, no physical presence
    - Handmade paper: Tactile, material, unique
    - Human need for materiality
    - Lesson: Physical media retains value despite digital convenience

11. **Time as Value**
    - Handmade: Time-intensive = value
    - Fast production: Time-saving = cheap
    - Different valuation systems
    - Lesson: Speed not always value (sometimes it's cost)

12. **Craft as Cultural Heritage**
    - Papermaking: Not just technique but cultural expression
    - UNESCO recognition: Washi as intangible heritage
    - Living traditions: Craft as cultural continuity
    - Lesson: Technology embodies culture, not just utility
