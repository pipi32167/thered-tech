# Just Intonation and Modal Music Theory - The Harmonic Systems Before Equal Temperament

## Historical Development

### Greek Foundations (500 BCE-200 CE)

**Pythagorean Discoveries**
Mathematical foundations (6th century BCE):
- **String ratios**: Discovering octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), fourth (4:3) ratios
- **Tetractys**: Sacred symbol representing consonant intervals
- **Cosmic music**: Music of the spheres concept
- **Mathematical beauty**: Belief in universe's mathematical harmony
- **Monochord**: Single-string instrument for precise interval measurement

**Greek Genera**
Three scale types:
- **Diatonic**: Whole and half tones (similar to modern major/minor)
- **Chromatic**: Predominantly minor thirds
- **Enharmonic**: Quarter tones (mostly lost to modern practice)
- **Ethos theory**: Different modes producing different emotional effects
- **Musical education**: Music central to Greek education and philosophy

**Aristoxenus** (4th century BCE)
Empirical approach:
- **Ear-based**: Relying on听觉 rather than mathematical ratios
- **Continuous pitch**: Pitch as continuum rather than discrete ratios
- **Aesthetic judgment**: Musical judgment based on auditory perception
- **Opposition to Pythagoreans**: Challenging purely mathematical approach
- **Lost works**: Most writings lost, known through later references

### Early Christian and Medieval Periods (200-1400 CE)

**Byzantine Octoechos**
Eight-mode system:
- **Echos**: Eight church modes (four authentic, four plagal)
- **Octoechos**: System of eight modes organizing liturgical chant
- **Centonization**: Composing from traditional melodic formulas
- **Oral transmission**: Melodies transmitted orally before notation
- **Liturgical integration**: Modes integrated with church calendar and texts

**Gregorian Chant Modes**
Western church modes:
- **Four authentic modes**: Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian
- **Four plagal modes**: Hypodorian, Hypophrygian, Hypolydian, Hypomixolydian
- **Finalis**: Each mode's reciting tone
- **Ambitus**: Each mode's range
- **Psalmody**: Chanting psaltes in modal system

**Guido of Arezzo** (1000 CE)
Musical innovations:
- **Staff notation**: Inventing lined notation for precise pitch indication
- **Solmization**: Ut-re-mi-fa-sol-la system (originally six notes)
- **Hexachords**: Six-note scale segments for teaching singing
- **Musical education**: Making music literacy more accessible
- **Theoretical writings**: Micrologus and other theoretical works

**Medieval Polyphony**
Developing complexity:
- **Organum**: Early parallel polyphony (9th-12th centuries)
- **Notre Dame School**: Léonin and Pérotin's organum (12th-13th centuries)
- **Consonance**: Perfect consonances (octave, fifth, fourth) only
- **Rhythmic modes**: Early rhythmic notation systems
- **Modal rhythm**: Rhythmic patterns based on modal theory

### Renaissance Peak (1400-1600 CE)

**Modal System Maturity**
Renaissance polyphony:
- **Sixteen modes**: Expanded from eight to sixteen modes (Glareanus, 1547)
- **Modal variety**: Rich modal variety in sacred and secular music
- **Cross-relations**: Modal mixture creating expressive cross-relations
- **Cadential formulas**: Each mode's characteristic cadences
- **Compositional techniques**: Modal counterpoint and imitation

**Just Intonation Theory**
Renaissance theorists:
- **Gioseffo Zarlino** (1558): Le istituzioni harmoniche, defining just intervals
- **Syntonic comma**: Recognizing the comma (81:80) problem in just intonation
- **Major triad**: 4:5:6 ratios for major triad
- **Minor triad**: 10:12:15 ratios for minor triad
- **Natural harmony**: Just intervals as "natural" consonances

** meantone Temperament**
Compromise system:
- **Quarter-comma meantone**: Tempering fifths to favor pure thirds
- **Wolf interval**: One very discordant fifth (G♯-E♭)
- **Key limitations**: Some keys unusable due to wolf interval
- **Widely adopted**: Dominant Renaissance tuning
- **Expressive limitations**: Composers avoiding keys with wolf

**Compositional Mastery**
Renaissance achievements:
- **Palestrina** (1525-1594): Peak of Renaissance polyphony
- **Victoria, Lassus, Byrd**: Modal polyphonic masters
- **Counterpoint**: Sophisticated imitative counterpoint
- **Text expression**: Music expressing text meaning and emotion
- **Modal purity**: Maintaining modal integrity while developing complexity

### Baroque Transition (1600-1750 CE)

**Transition to Major-Minor**
Gradual system change:
- **Modal decline**: Modes gradually replaced by major/minor
- **Functional harmony**: Developing functional harmony (tonic, dominant, subdominant)
- **Chord progressions**: Root movement by fifths becoming standard
- **Modulatory practice**: Increasing use of modulation to distant keys
- **Expressive range**: Major/minor system allowing greater expressive range

**Well-Temperament**
Multiple temperaments:
- **Werckmeister** (1691): Well-tempered systems allowing all keys
- **Neidhardt** (1706): Various circulating temperaments
- **Bach's "Well-Tempered Clavier"** (1722, 1742): Demonstrating well-tempered possibilities
- **Key characteristics**: Each key having distinct character
- **Variety**: Multiple competing temperament systems

**Equal Temperament Emergence**
Gradual standardization:
- **Theoretical proposals**: Various theorists proposing equal division of octave
- **Practical adoption**: Keyboard makers adopting equal temperament gradually
- **Resistance**: Composers and musicians resisting equal temperament's harsh thirds
- **Organ building**: Organs among last to adopt equal temperament
- **National differences**: Different countries adopting at different rates

### Classical and Romantic Periods (1750-1900 CE)

**Equal Temperament Triumph**
Complete standardization:
- **Piano repertoire**: Beethoven, Chopin, Liszt requiring all keys equally usable
- **Orchestral transposition**: Orchestral music requiring free transposition
- **Chromaticism**: Increasing chromaticism requiring equal temperament
- **Modulatory distance**: Remote modulations requiring all keys equally available
- **Instrument manufacturing**: Standardized manufacturing requiring standard tuning

**Theory Codification**
Complete major-minor system:
- **Rameau** (1722): Traité de l'harmonie, establishing functional harmony
- **Riemann** (1890s): Developing harmonic theory fully
- **Textbooks**: Music theory textbooks teaching major-minor system
- **Ear training**: Conservatories teaching equal temperament as natural
- **Historical forgetting**: Just intonation and modal system largely forgotten

**Complete Displacement**
By late 19th century:
- **Concert pitch**: Standardization of A=440 Hz (1939)
- **Orchestral tuning**: All orchestras using equal temperament
- **Piano dominance**: Piano as central instrument, always equal tempered
- **Conservatory curriculum**: Equal temperament exclusively taught
- **Harmonic language**: All contemporary harmony based on equal temperament

## Technical Principles

### Just Intonation Intervals

**Perfect Consonances**
Most stable intervals:
- **Unison**: 1:1 (exact same pitch)
- **Octave**: 2:1 (doubling frequency)
- **Perfect fifth**: 3:2 (most stable after octave)
- **Perfect fourth**: 4:3 (less stable than fifth)
- **Acoustical purity**: Vibrations aligning perfectly, "beating" minimized

**Imperfect Consonances**
Less stable but pleasant:
- **Major third**: 5:4 (bright, stable)
- **Minor third**: 6:5 (darker, more tense)
- **Major sixth**: 5:3 (brighter than sixth)
- **Minor sixth**: 8:5 (darker)
- **Suspension quality**: Imperfect consonances create tension-resolution patterns

**Dissonances**
Requiring resolution:
- **Major second**: 9:8 or 10:9 (two sizes in just intonation!)
- **Minor second**: 16:15 (very tense)
- **Major seventh**: 15:8 (extremely tense, leading to octave)
- **Minor seventh**: 9:5 or 16:9 (again, two sizes)
- **Resolution imperative**: Dissonances creating expectation of resolution

**Problem of Commas**
Tuning contradictions:
- **Syntonic comma**: 81:80 (difference between four perfect fifths and two octaves + major third)
- **Pythagorean comma**: 531441:524288 (twelve fifths vs. seven octaves)
- **Diesis**: 128:125 (three major thirds vs. octave)
- ** commas** means "must decide": Impossible to have all intervals pure
- **Tuning compromises**: Must choose which intervals to compromise

### Modal System Structure

**Authentic and Plagal Modes**
Two ranges for each final:
- **Authentic modes**: Final at bottom of range (Dorian: D-D')
- **Plagal modes**: Final in middle of range (Hypodorian: A-D')
- **Reciting tone**: Tenor note around which melody revolves
- **Ambitus**: Range of each mode
- **Species differentiation**: Differentiating mode species by range

**Eight Church Modes**
Medieval system:
1. **Dorian**: D-D (authentic) / A-D (plagal)
2. **Phrygian**: E-E (authentic) / B-E (plagal)
3. **Lydian**: F-F (authentic) / C-F (plagal)
4. **Mixolydian**: G-G (authentic) / D-G (plagal)
5. **Hypodorian**: A-D (plagal of Dorian)
6. **Hypophrygian**: B-E (plagal of Phrygian)
7. **Hypolydian**: C-F (plagal of Lydian)
8. **Hypomixolydian**: D-G (plagal of Mixolydian)

**Renaissance Expansion**
Twelve-mode system:
- **Adding four modes**: Adding Ionian and Hypoionian, Aeolian and Hypoaeolian
- **Glareanus (1547)**: Dodecachordon expanding system
- **Final on A**: Aeolian mode (precursor to natural minor)
- **Final on C**: Ionian mode (precursor to major)
- **Transition**: Mediating between modal and major-minor systems

**Modal Characteristics**
Each mode's distinctive qualities:
- **Interval pattern**: Each mode's characteristic whole/half tone pattern
- **Melodic tendencies**: Characteristic melodic formulas and cadences
- **Emotional associations**: Medieval ethos theory attributing emotional qualities
- **Psalm tones**: Each mode's psalm formulas
- **Contrasts**: Modal contrasts creating variety

### Temperament Systems

**Meantone Temperament**
Renaissance compromise:
- **Quarter-comma**: Tempering each fifth by 1/4 syntonic comma
- **Pure thirds**: Major thirds beatless and pure (5:4)
- **Compromised fifths**: Fifths narrow, beating
- **Wolf interval**: One very discordant fifth (usually G♯-E♭)
- **Key limitations**: Usable keys limited to those avoiding wolf

**Well-Temperament**
Baroque flexibility:
- **Circulating temperaments**: All keys usable but with distinct characters
- **Key color**: Each key having unique character due to interval variations
- **Multiple systems**: Werckmeister, Neidhardt, Vallotti, Young, others
- **Bach demonstration**: "Well-Tempered Clavier" demonstrating possibilities
- **Variety**: No single well-tempered system, multiple competing options

**Equal Temperament**
Modern standard:
- **Twelve equal semitones**: Each semitone exactly 100 cents
- **Fifths tempered**: Each fifth narrow by 2 cents (logarithmic)
- **Thirds wide**: Major thirds wide by about 14 cents (noticeably beating)
- **All keys identical**: Every key has same intervallic relationships
- **Compromise**: No intervals perfectly pure, but all usable

### Tuning Practice

**Pythagorean Tuning**
Oldest documented system:
- **Perfect fifths**: Tuning upward by perfect 3:2 fifths
- **Descending fourths**: Tuning downward by 4:3 fourths
- **Problem**: Thirds very wide (81:64, called "Pythagorean third")
- **Major thirds**: Dissonant and harsh
- **Historical use**: Medieval monophony and early polyphony

**Just Intonation in Practice**
Achieving pure intervals:
- **Harmonic generation**: Tuning from harmonic series
- **Tuning by ear**: Listening for beats to find pure intervals
- **Root progression**: Tuning root progressions in just intonation
- **Adaptive tuning**: Adjusting tuning for each key or chord
- **Practical challenges**: Requires constant adjustment or limited tonality

**Keyboard Tuning**
Practical compromises:
- **Starting note**: Usually beginning with C
- **Tuning sequence**: Specific order for tuning intervals
- **Beats**: Listening to beat rates to temper intervals
- **Check chords**: Verifying tuning through test chords
- **Registration**: Setting temperament within one octave, then extending

## Conclusion

Just intonation and modal music theory represent approximately 2500 years of Western musical practice, from Pythagorean mathematical discoveries through Greek modal theory, medieval church modes, Renaissance polyphonic mastery to Baroque transition and ultimate displacement by equal temperament and major-minor tonality.

The history encompasses:

**Mathematical Foundations**:
- **Pythagorean ratios**: Octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), fourth (4:3) as perfect consonances
- **Harmonic series**: Just intervals based on natural harmonic series
- **Integer ratios**: Simple ratios creating consonance (5:4 major third, 6:5 minor third)
- **Mathematical beauty**: Music reflecting cosmic mathematical order
- **Tuning systems**: Pythagorean, just intonation, meantone, well-tempered, equal temperament

**Modal System**:
- **Greek modes**: Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian modes
- **Church modes**: Eight medieval modes (four authentic, four plagal)
- **Renaissance expansion**: Twelve modes by Glareanus (1547)
- **Modal characteristics**: Each mode's distinctive intervallic pattern and melodic tendencies
- **Major-minor emergence**: Gradual transition from modal to major-minor system

**Renaissance Peak**:
- **Polyphonic mastery**: Palestrina, Victoria, Lassus, Byrd's modal polyphony
- **Zarlino's theory**: Just intonation theory (4:5:6 major triad, 10:12:15 minor triad)
- **Meantone temperament**: Quarter-comma meantone with pure thirds
- **Compositional complexity**: Sophisticated imitative counterpoint within modal framework
- **Expressive achievement**: Deep emotional and spiritual expression

**Baroque Transition**:
- **Major-minor emergence**: Gradual shift to functional major-minor system
- **Well-temperament**: Bach's "Well-Tempered Clavier" demonstrating circulating temperaments
- **Modulatory expansion**: Increasing use of remote modulation
- **Functional harmony**: Developing tonic-dominant-subdominant relationships
- **Equal temperament emergence**: Gradual standardization on equal temperament

**Complete Displacement** (1750-1900):
- **Equal temperament triumph**: Standardization by late 19th century
- **Orchestral standardization**: All orchestras adopting equal temperament
- **Piano repertoire**: Beethoven, Chopin, Liszt requiring all keys equally usable
- **Conservatory curriculum**: Equal temperament exclusively taught
- **Historical forgetting**: Just intonation and modal system largely forgotten

**Contemporary Revival**:
- **Early music movement**: Historical performances on historical tunings
- **Just intonation composers**: Partch, Johnston, Riley, Young creating new music
- **World music influence**: Global awareness of alternative tuning systems
- **Acoustical research**: Scientific study of consonance perception
- **Digital possibilities**: Electronic instruments capable of any tuning system

The story of just intonation and modal theory offers insights about:
- **Standardization's costs**: Equal temperament's convenience sacrificing intervallic purity and key diversity
- **Loss of diversity**: Reduction from many modes and key characters to standardized uniformity
- **Perceptual flexibility**: What sounds "natural" is learned through exposure, not biologically fixed
- **Technological momentum**: Equal temperament's lock-in through manufacturing, education, repertoire
- **Artistic compromise**: Music shaped by technological constraints and possibilities
- **Value of alternatives**: Multiple valid approaches to musical organization, not single correct system

The pure interval's resonance, beating-free and warm, compared to equal temperament's beating thirds; the distinctive character of each key in meantone or well-temperament compared to equal temperament's uniformity; the richness of modal variety compared to major-minor's duality—these represent not historical curiosities but alternative musical possibilities offering different expressive resources, aesthetic experiences, and relationships to acoustic nature.

Equal temperament's triumph represents genuine progress in modulatory freedom, instrumental practicality, and theoretical simplicity, but also loss of:
- **Intervallic purity**: Pure intervals' unique resonant quality
- **Key character**: Distinctive character of each key
- **Modal variety**: Richness of modal contrasts
- **Historical repertoire**: Historical music sounding as composers intended
- **Acoustic connection**: Music's connection to natural harmonic series

In an era of equal temperament's ubiquity, just intonation and modal theory's revival—through early music performance, contemporary composition, world music influence, or electronic experimentation—demonstrates musical diversity's value, maintains alternative musical possibilities, and offers reminder that standardization, while pragmatic, represents compromise rather than perfection.

The harpsichord tuned in quarter-comma meantone, its pure thirds resonating; the choir tuning pure chords by ear, the perfect alignment of harmonics; the distinctive character of D minor vs. D major in meantone temperament—these historical practices represent not just lost techniques but alternative relationships between music, nature, and human perception that equal temperament's standardization has largely eliminated from Western music.

Their continued practice and revival—whether in historical performance, contemporary composition, world music, or experimental exploration—maintains musical diversity, offering alternatives to equal temperament's uniformity and reminders of music's capacity for multiple valid systems of organization, each with different strengths, limitations, and aesthetic qualities.
