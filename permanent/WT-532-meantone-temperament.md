# WT-532: Meantone Temperament

## Domain
Music Theory, Acoustics, Tuning Systems, Historical Musicology

## Brief History
Meantone temperament was a tuning system for keyboard instruments (harpsichords, organs, clavichords) prevalent from the 16th through 18th centuries. Unlike modern equal temperament (which uses 12 equally spaced semitones), meantone prioritized pure thirds (major and minor) by compromising the fifths. This created exquisitely consonant harmony in certain keys but rendered other keys unusable due to "wolf intervals" (dissonant, beating intervals). Meantone enabled Renaissance and Baroque music's characteristic harmonic language—particularly the emphasis on major thirds and the emotional power of harmony. However, as music became more chromatic and modulatory in the late 18th century, meantone's limitations became intolerable. Equal temperament, with its compromise of all intervals but usability in all keys, gradually replaced meantone. By the mid-19th century, meantone was largely abandoned except in historical performance practice. The system represents both the pursuit of acoustic perfection and the trade-offs inherent in musical systems, offering insights into historical performance practice and the mathematics of musical harmony.

## Comprehensive Analysis

### 1. Technological Overview and Core Principles

#### 1.1 Musical Acoustics Fundamentals

**The Harmonic Series:**
When a note is played, it produces overtones at multiples of the fundamental frequency:
- Fundamental: f
- 2nd harmonic (octave): 2f
- 3rd harmonic (fifth): 3f
- 4th harmonic (octave): 4f
- 5th harmonic (major third): 5f

**Interval Ratios:**
- **Octave:** 2:1 (exactly)
- **Perfect Fifth:** 3:2 (exactly in nature)
- **Perfect Fourth:** 4:3 (exactly in nature)
- **Major Third:** 5:4 (exactly in nature)
- **Minor Third:** 6:5 (exactly in nature)

**The Challenge:**
Keyboard instruments have 12 notes per octave, but natural intervals don't divide evenly into 12. You can't tune a keyboard so all intervals are pure—some compromise is necessary.

#### 1.2 The Meantone Solution

**Core Principle:**
Make the major third pure (5:4 ratio) by narrowing the fifths slightly from their natural 3:2 ratio.

**Calculation:**
- Natural fifth: 3:2 = 1.5
- Pure major third: 5:4 = 1.25
- Four fifths stacked should equal a major third plus two octaves: (3/2)^4 = 5:4 × 4
- This means: (3/2)^4 = 5/1? Let's calculate...
- (3/2) × (3/2) × (3/2) × (3/2) = 81/16
- We want: 5/1 (which is 5:4 × 4)
- So we need: fifth = (5/4)^(1/4) = fifth root of 5/4

**The Meantone Fifth:**
- Fifth ratio: 5^(1/4):2 ≈ 1.49535 (vs natural 1.5)
- Slightly narrower than natural fifth
- This deviation is called the syntonic comma

**The Result:**
- Major thirds: perfectly pure (5:4)
- Minor thirds: perfectly pure (6:5)
- Fifths: slightly narrowed from natural
- Whole tones: consistent (two different sizes depending on specific meantone variant)
- Semitones: consistent (two different sizes depending on specific meantone variant)

#### 1.3 Meantone Variants

**Quarter-Comma Meantone (Most Common):**
- Fifth narrowed by 1/4 of the syntonic comma
- Major third: perfectly pure (5:4)
- Minor third: perfectly pure (6:5)
- Fifth: 697 cents (vs pure 702 cents)
- Wolf fifth: significantly dissonant (about 678 cents or 738 cents depending on which fifth is left wide)

**Fifth-Comma Meantone:**
- Fifth narrowed by 1/5 of the syntonic comma
- Better fifths, slightly compromised thirds
- More usable keys than quarter-comma meantone
- Historically less common

**Third-Comma Meantone:**
- Fifth narrowed by 1/3 of the syntonic comma
- Thirds nearly pure but not exactly
- Historical variants documented
- Rarely used

#### 1.4 Keyboard Layout and Implementation

**Musical Consequences:**

**Good Keys (Usable):**
- C major: excellent
- G major: excellent
- D major: good
- F major: good
- Bb major: good to acceptable
- Minor keys parallel to above: excellent to good

**Problematic Keys (Barely Usable):**
- Keys with many accidentals (A major, E major, B major, etc.)
- Keys requiring more than 3-4 sharps or flats
- Keys involving the "wolf fifth"

**The Wolf Interval:**
- One fifth must be left wide to maintain octaves
- In C meantone: typically G#-Eb (or D#-Bb depending on tuning)
- This "wolf fifth" beats audibly and is unusable
- Limits modulation to keys avoiding this interval

**Practical Keyboard:**
- Chromatic keyboard (12 notes per octave)
- Accidentals (sharps/flats) tuned to meantone system
- Harpsichords and organs built specifically for meantone
- Cannot play in all keys
- Limited modulation range

### 2. Historical Development and Evolution

#### 2.1 Origins and Early Development (Late 15th - Early 16th Century)

**Pre-Meantone Tuning:**
- **Pythagorean Tuning:** Perfect fifths (3:2), very impure thirds (81:64)
- **Just Intonation:** Pure intervals, but not practical for fixed tuning
- **Temperament Need:** Keyboard instruments required compromise tuning

**Renaissance Music Requirements:**
- **Vertical Harmony:** Renaissance music emphasized vertical harmony (simultaneous notes)
- **Thirds Became Important:** Major and minor thirds became consonant intervals
- **Harmonic Language:** Music based on triadic harmony (thirds and fifths)
- **Emotional Expression:** Composers wanted expressive harmony

**Meantone Emergence:**
- **Pietro Aron:** Early theorist discussing meantone (1523)

- **Practical Adoption:** Tuners and keyboard builders adopted meantone principles

### 3. Technical Analysis and Musical Implications

#### 3.1 Acoustic Characteristics

**Pure Intervals:**
- **Major Thirds:** Perfectly pure (5:4 ratio, 386 cents)
- **Minor Thirds:** Perfectly pure (6:5 ratio, 316 cents)
- **Fifths:** Slightly narrowed (697 cents in quarter-comma)
- **Fourths:** Correspondingly widened from pure due to octave maintenance
- **Sixths:** Pure major sixth (5:3) and minor sixth (8:5)

**Wolf Interval:**
- **Dissonant Fifth:** One significantly impure fifth
- **Beating:** Strong, rapid beating between component tones
- **Unusable:** Cannot be used melodically or harmonically
- **Limits Modulation:** Limits modulation away from this interval
- **Placement:** Typically G#-Db or D#-Ab depending on tuning

**Interval Sizes (Quarter-Comma Meantone):**
- **Whole Tone:** 193 cents (larger than equal temperament 200 cents)
- **Semitone:** 117 cents (narrower than equal temperament 100 cents)
- **Minor Third:** 310 cents (pure 6:5, slightly less than equal temperament 300 cents)
- **Major Third:** 386 cents (pure 5:4, less than equal temperament 400 cents)
- **Perfect Fifth:** 697 cents (narrower than pure 702 cents)

#### 3.2 Musical Consequences

**Harmonic Richness:**
- **Chord Quality:** Triads with pure thirds have exceptional clarity and resonance
- **Harmonic Series Alignment:** Better alignment with natural harmonic series
- **Resonance:** Better resonance when intervals align with natural harmonics
- **Beating:** Minimal beating in pure intervals (more focused sound)
- **Tonal Center:** Stronger tonal center due to pure intervals

**Key Limitations:**
- **Usable Keys:** Approximately 6-8 major keys fully usable
- **Modulation Range:** Limited modulation without encountering wolf interval
- **Chromaticism:** Limited chromaticism without dissonance
- **Distant Keys:** Keys distant from C major require wolf interval
- **Modulatory Distance:** Limited modulatory range

**Compositional Consequences:**
- **Key Choice:** Composers chose keys within meantone's usable range
- **Modulation Strategy:** Careful modulation avoiding wolf intervals
- **Harmonic Emphasis:** Emphasis on beautifully tuned major thirds
- **Chord Progressions:** Progressions avoiding problematic intervals
- **Modulatory Vocabulary:** Limited modulatory vocabulary compared to later music

#### 3.3 Comparison with Alternative Temperaments

**vs Pythagorean Tuning:**
- **Pythagorean:** Pure fifths (702 cents), very impure thirds (408 cents)
- **Meantone:** Slightly compromised fifths (697 cents), pure thirds (386 cents)
- **Advantage:** Meantone had beautiful thirds, Pythagorean thirds very dissonant
- **Trade-off:** Slight fifth compromise for major third purity

**vs Equal Temperament:**
- **Equal Temperament:** All intervals equally usable, all keys equally usable
- **Meantone:** Some intervals pure (thirds), some compromised (fifths), one unusable (wolf)
- **Advantage:** Meantone had beautiful thirds in usable keys
- **Trade-off:** Key limitation for harmonic purity

**vs Well-Temperament:**
- **Well-Temperament:** All keys usable, intervals unequally pure
- **Meantone:** Some keys unusable, intervals in good keys very pure
- **Advantage:** Well-temperament offered key variety
- **Trade-off:** Meantone offered superior purity in limited keys

### 7. Lessons and Insights

#### 7.1 The Pursuit of Perfection vs Practicality

**Perfectionism's Cost:**
- **Pursuit of Purity:** Meantone pursued purity of thirds (5:4 ratio)
- **Cost of Purity:** Cost was key limitation (wolf interval, limited keys)
- **Practical Trade-offs:** Practical limitations constrained perfection pursuit
- **Ultimate Compromise:** Equal temperament compromised all intervals for practicality
- **Lesson:** Perfection pursuit often involves practical limitations and trade-offs

**Practical Triumph:**
- **Practical Over Pure:** Practical flexibility triumphed over interval purity
- **Flexibility Value:** Key flexibility and modulatory freedom valued
- **Usability Trumps Purity:** Usability in all keys more valuable than purity in some keys
- **Artistic Consequences:** Artistic practice adapted to practical system

**Contemporary Relevance:**
- **Quality vs Compromise:** Balancing quality and compromise in technology
- **Flexibility vs Optimization:** Flexibility often valued over optimization
- **System Design:** Designing systems for practical use vs pure optimization
- **Requirements Analysis:** Understanding requirements before system design
- **Trade-off Management:** Managing trade-offs in system design

**Lesson:**
Meantone illustrates the classic pattern of perfectionism vs practicality. Meantone pursued acoustic perfection (pure thirds), but the cost (wolf interval, limited keys) proved impractical as musical demands evolved. This pattern repeats throughout technology: systems optimized for one ideal often prove inadequate when requirements broaden or change. Practical flexibility often trumps optimization, compromise often beats perfection, and systems that enable rather than constrain tend to prevail in the long run.

#### 8.6 Final Reflection

Meantone temperament represented the pursuit of acoustic perfection in keyboard tuning—a sophisticated mathematical solution that prioritized the purity of major and minor thirds (5:4 and 6:5 ratios) by slightly compromising the perfect fifths. This created exquisitely consonant harmony in approximately 6-8 major keys, enabling the rich harmonic language of Renaissance and Baroque music with its emphasis on triadic harmony and vertical sonority.

For over two centuries, meantone was the standard tuning for keyboard instruments in Europe. Harpsichords and organs were built specifically for meantone, composers wrote music within meantone's constraints (avoiding wolf intervals, limiting modulation, choosing favorable keys), and musicians developed aural sensitivity to meantone's pure thirds and characteristic key colors. The system enabled the harmonic richness and emotional power that defined Renaissance and Baroque music, creating a distinctive musical language that exploited meantone's strengths while working within its limitations.

However, musical evolution in the late 18th century demanded more than meantone could provide. Composers wanted chromaticism, distant modulation, and full key variety for dramatic and expressive purposes. The Classical style's emphasis on variety and flexibility, followed by the Romantic period's expansion of harmonic and modulatory range, required the key freedom and modulation flexibility that only equal temperament could provide.

The transition to equal temperament was a victory of practical flexibility over acoustic perfection. Equal temperament compromised all intervals slightly (each semitone exactly 100 cents) to achieve key freedom and modulation freedom, enabling unlimited chromaticism and free modulation. This compromise made all keys equally usable, allowed complete harmonic and modulatory freedom, and enabled the expanded expressive range demanded by evolving musical aesthetics.

The complete triumph of equal temperament in mainstream music created universal compatibility and key freedom but also involved genuine losses: the acoustic purity and harmonic richness of meantone, the distinct character and color of different keys, the connection to historical sound and practice, and the diversity of tuning systems. Western music became universally tunable and modulatable but homogenized in tuning.

The contemporary revival of meantone in historical performance practice demonstrates that what was lost still has value. When Renaissance and Baroque music is performed on meantone instruments in meantone tuning, the harmonic richness, key color, and acoustic clarity are striking and beautiful. The purity of thirds creates resonance and clarity that equal temperament cannot achieve, even though meantone's limitations are also apparent.

The story of meantone is the story of the pursuit of perfection and its sacrifice for flexibility. It shows how technical systems designed for specific requirements can become inadequate when requirements expand beyond the system's capabilities. It illustrates the diversity cost of standardization: equal temperament's universal compatibility came at the cost of acoustic purity and diversity.

It also demonstrates how artistic practice is shaped by technical systems, and how technical change drives artistic evolution. Composers and musicians created beautiful music within meantone's constraints, developing musical language that exploited meantone's strengths. When the technical system changed (equal temperament), musical practice evolved to exploit new possibilities, creating new musical languages (Classical, Romantic) that would have been impossible in meantone.

In our age of digital technology and algorithmic recommendation engines that optimize for engagement and convenience, meantone's story offers a different perspective: the value of acoustic purity and harmonic perfection, even at the cost of flexibility and variety. It suggests that optimization for flexibility and universality isn't always optimal—that sometimes choosing a system optimized for specific, focused excellence (even with limitations) can produce results worth preserving.

The historical performance movement's revival of meantone demonstrates that what was lost can be partially recovered, that specialized knowledge can be reconstructed from fragmentary evidence, and that historical systems can have value in contemporary contexts. Meantone survives as a heritage practice, a living connection to historical musical practice, and an example of acoustic perfection pursued and achieved.

Meantone reminds us that all systems involve trade-offs, that optimization for one goal often compromises another, that standardization creates benefits but also costs, and that diversity—even in technical systems—has value that shouldn't be casually discarded for the convenience of universality. It represents both the human pursuit of acoustic perfection and the practical necessity of compromise in service of expanding musical possibilities.
