# Toll Bridge Systems and Private Infrastructure (收费桥梁制度)

## 中文摘要

🏛️ **技术起源与基本原理**
收费桥梁制度起源于12世纪欧洲，是一种通过向过桥用户收取费用来回收建设成本并维护基础设施的融资模式。其核心原理包括：**私人融资**（私人资本建造桥梁，通过收费回收投资）、**使用权交易**（桥梁服务商品化，使用者付费）、**长期维护**（持续收费支持维护保养）、**特许经营**（王室或政府授予收费特许权）。关键技术要素：**收费关卡**（收费亭、收费员、收费表）、**收费机制**（按次、按月、年票、季节性差异化定价）、**结构设计**（便于收费的狭窄桥面、防御逃费的设计）、**法律框架**（收费权契约、收费标准制定、纠纷解决）。该技术代表了前现代时期基础设施融资和管理的创新模式，使私人资本能够参与公共基础设施建设，解决了政府财政不足问题，催生了现代公私合营(PPP)模式的雏形，但其作为公共服务被收费化的属性最终被免费通行桥隧所取代。

📜 **历史发展与社会影响**
中世纪起源(1100-1500)：法国12世纪开始出现收费桥梁、意大利威尼斯里亚托桥(1181)早期收费、英国伦敦桥(1209)收费维持、桥梁行会(Carpenters' Company 13-14世纪)运营桥梁收费、教会和贵族建造桥梁并收费(慈善+投资回报)。近代发展(1500-1800)：收费公路信托(Turnpike Trusts 1663英国收费公路)扩展到桥梁、美国殖民地收费桥梁(18世纪)、法国路易十四时期大规模桥梁建设+收费、普鲁士国营收费桥梁系统。黄金时代(1800-1870)：工业革命交通需求激增、收费桥梁公司成立(英国、美国)、基础设施投资热潮、1825年Stockton & Darlington铁路需要过桥收费、城市化加速桥梁需求。衰落期(1870-1950)：公共所有权理念兴起(桥梁应是免费公共品)、铁路公司收购收费桥(铁路运输一体化)、市政当局收购(19世纪末-20世纪初)、技术进步(更大跨度桥梁、更多免费替代)、汽车时代需求(新桥建设、免费通行压力)。社会影响：(1) **融资模式**：私人资本参与基础设施、不增加税收、风险私人承担。(2) **区域发展**：收费桥梁促进贸易、连接被河流/峡谷分隔的社区、催生城镇。(3) **社会分层**：富人付得起、穷人绕远路或等待免费时段、 resentment积累。(4) **法律制度**：特许经营权法律、收费标准制定、逃费处罚、纠纷解决机制形成。(5) **现代PPP起源**：私人建设-运营-移交(BOOT)模式原型、政府监管与私人运营结合。

⚰️ **衰落与消亡**
收费桥梁制度的衰落分五个阶段。(1) **公共所有权理念**(1870s-1900)：桥梁作为公共品观念兴起、免费通行成为政治诉求、市政当局通过收购实现"免费桥梁"。(2) **技术替代**(1870-1920)：更大跨度桥梁、更多替代路线、桥梁数量增加降低绕路成本、汽车时代路网扩张。(3) **政府收购**(1880-1950)：市政债券、税收资助收购私人收费桥、伦敦桥梁公司收购(1905-1920s)、美国收费桥逐步转为免费。(4) **高速公路时代**(1930-1980)：免费公共高速公路、收费桥隧被免费公路取代、收费限于新的大型桥隧项目(例外情况)。(5) **交通一体化**(1950-2000)：交通网络整体规划、区域性通行费代替单点收费、电子收费系统。关键转折：英国1905年伦敦桥梁公司法案(市政收购)、美国1930s大萧条时期收费桥收购、二战后欧洲免费桥梁政策、1960s美国州际高速公路免费通行。技术断层的形成：私人融资公共基础设施知识1970s基本消失、收费关卡设计和运营管理(收费亭布局、收费员培训、假币识别)1940s失传、收费权契约法律框架(特许经营权、收费标准制定、纠纷解决)1970s被政府采购法取代、长期维护收费模式(而非短期收费还本)1950s观念转变。当代例外：大型现代化桥隧(隧道、桥梁)恢复收费模式(电子收费)、但基于"拥挤管理"而非"还本"逻辑。

💡 **当代启示与现代应用**
21世纪收费基础设施经历了"概念复兴"而非"直接恢复"。(1) **拥挤定价**：新加坡道路定价(1975)、伦敦拥堵费(2003)、斯德哥尔摩拥堵税(2007)、基于经济学的交通管理(需求高峰收费、空闲时段免费)。(2) **大型基础设施**：英法海底隧道(1994)收费还贷、桥梁隧道PPP项目(中国收费公路 1990s-2020s)、BOT/BOOT模式(建设-运营-移交)、私人资本参与基础设施。(3) **数字基础设施**：网络 neutrality争议(互联网分级定价风险)、平台服务分级定价(高级服务收费)、数字基础设施"收费站"(data caps, overage fees)、政府监管平衡普遍接入与投资激励。(4) **遗产保护**：历史收费桥转为旅游景点(伦敦塔桥、布鲁克林大桥收费史展览)、收费小屋博物馆、收费制度历史展示、工业遗产保护。(5) **公私合营2.0**：联合国推广PPP模式、基础设施投资需求(全球每年数万亿美元)、政府财政压力推动PPP、风险管理、合同设计、监管框架。技术哲学启示：公共服务"免费"实际上税收资助(隐形收费)、拥挤定价将外部性内部化(交通拥堵社会成本)、基础设施融资多样化(税收、使用费、公私合营)、私人运营效率vs公共公平平衡、收费理念演变：从"还本"到"拥挤管理"(收费目的根本转变)。

## English Analysis

## I. Introduction: When Infrastructure Was a Business, Not a Right

### A. The Thesis: Toll Bridges as Proto-PPP and the Commodification of Transportation

Toll bridge systems represent one of history's most innovative yet controversial infrastructure financing models: the transformation of public works from purely governmental responsibilities into profit-generating enterprises. Developed in medieval Europe (12th century onwards) and flourishing during the 18th and 19th centuries, toll bridges enabled private capital to build infrastructure that governments could not or would not fund, creating networks of bridges, roads, and canals that connected communities and facilitated commerce. The gradual displacement of toll bridges by publicly funded, free-access infrastructure in the late 19th and 20th centuries illustrates shifting social philosophies about the role of government, the nature of public goods, and citizens' relationship to public services. What makes this transformation particularly significant is not just the disappearance of tolls but the fundamental reimagining of infrastructure as a public right rather than a commodity. The contemporary revival of tolling in the form of congestion pricing, electronic toll collection, and public-private partnerships demonstrates that while the specific historical model of toll bridges has disappeared, the underlying principles of user fees, cost recovery, and value capture remain highly relevant in contemporary infrastructure policy.

### B. Historical Context and Timeline

**Medieval Origins (1100-1500 CE)**:

**Early European toll bridges**:
- **France**: 12th-century toll bridges emerge
- **Italy**: Rialto Bridge, Venice (1181)
  - **Reconstruction**: Stone bridge replacing wooden
  - **Toll**: Funded and maintained through tolls
  - **Operation**: Bridge builders and operators
- **England**: Old London Bridge (1209)
  - **Builder**: Peter de Colechurch
  - **Funding**: Bridge Chapel rents, tolls
  - **Operation**: London Bridge Trust

**Church and noble patronage**:
- **Motivation**: Charity + investment return
- **Church bridge building**: Religious merit + economic return
- **Feudal obligations**: Bridge maintenance as feudal duty

**Early Modern Period (1500-1800)**:

**Turnpike expansion**:
- **England**: Turnpike trusts (1663 onwards)
  - **Roads**: Improved by trusts, funded by tolls
  - **Model**: Extended to bridges on turnpike roads
- **France**: 17th-18th century toll bridges
  - **Royal initiative**: Crown-sponsored construction
  - **Tolls**: User fees for maintenance

**American colonies**:
- **18th century**: Toll bridges common
  - **Charter**: Colonial governments granted charters
  - **Funding**: Private investment, tolls
  - **Examples**: Connecticut River toll bridges (1790s)

**Golden Age (1800-1870)**:

**Industrial Revolution demand**:
- **Transportation**: Growing need for infrastructure
- **Trade**: Commercial routes require bridges
- **Urbanization**: Cities expand, need connections

**Toll bridge companies**:
- **Stock companies**: Formed to build and operate toll bridges
- **Investment**: Private capital, shareholder profits
- **Dividends**: Toll revenue distributed to investors

**Railroad era integration**:
- **1825**: Stockton & Darlington Railway (first public railway)
  - **Toll bridges**: Railway must pay tolls on bridges
  - **Negotiation**: Volume discounts, season tickets
- **Conflict**: Railroads dislike tolls (operational costs)

**Decline and Displacement (1870-1950)**:

**Public ownership movement**:
- **Philosophy**: Infrastructure as public good
- **Politics**: "Free bridges" political demand
- **Municipal action**: Cities/taxing authority purchase toll bridges

**Technological factors**:
- **Larger spans**: Fewer bridges needed
- **Alternative routes**: More free crossings
- **Bridge quantity**: Increased supply reduces toll value

**Government acquisition**:
- **United Kingdom**: London Bridge Company acquisition (1905-1920s)
- **United States**: Municipal purchases (1890s-1940s)
- **Europe**: Gradual municipalization

**Twentieth-century transformation**:
- **Automobiles**: Increased demand for infrastructure
- **Highways**: Free public highways (1930s-1960s)
- **Toll tunnels**: Exceptions (some tunnels retained tolls)

## II. Technical and Economic Principles

### A. Toll Bridge Business Model

**Revenue structure**:

**Toll types**:
- **Per crossing**: Each time a vehicle/pedestrian crosses
- **Seasonal passes**: Monthly, annual tickets
- **Volume discounts**: For regular users (businesses, farmers)
- **Vehicle type pricing**: Higher for heavy vehicles (more bridge wear)

**Cost structure**:
- **Capital costs**: Bridge construction, tollhouse construction
- **Operating costs**: Toll collectors, maintenance, repairs
- **Financing costs**: Interest on capital
- **Profit**: Return to investors

**Rate setting**:
- **Fixed costs**: Recovered through base toll
- **Variable costs**: Heavier vehicles pay more (wear and tear)
- **Demand sensitivity**: Lower rates at off-peak times
- **Competition**: Alternate free routes affect pricing power

**Investment models**:

**Pure private**:
- **Investors**: Fund entire construction
- **Risk**: Investors bear all risk
- **Reward**: Toll revenue (hopefully) exceeds costs
- **Sale**: May sell to government later

**Public-private mixed**:
- **Subsidies**: Government provides some funding
- **Guarantee**: Minimum revenue guarantees
- **Risk sharing**: Both public and private bear risk

**Charters and concessions**:
- **Monopoly**: Exclusive right to collect tolls
- **Duration**: Often 20-99 years
- **Obligations**: Maintain bridge to specified standards
- **Rates**: Maximum tolls specified in charter

### B. Toll Collection Infrastructure and Operations

**Physical infrastructure**:

**Tollhouse/plaza**:
- **Location**: At one or both ends of bridge
- **Design**: Narrowing to force traffic past collection point
- **Size**: Varies from small booth to large plaza

**Collection mechanisms**:
- **Manual**: Toll collector at gate
- **Barrier**: Gate or boom (opens after payment)
- **Recording**: Toll book, ledger

**Security measures**:
- **Barriers**: Prevent toll evasion
- **Toll collectors**: Armed (sometimes), enforcement
- **Penalties**: Fines for evasion
- **Court prosecution**: Legal enforcement

**Operational challenges**:
- **Congestion**: Toll collection causes delays
- **Evasion**: People bypass tolls (swim across, go around)
- **Collection costs**: Toll collectors' wages reduce revenue
- **Counterfeiting**: Fake tokens, coins

### C. Legal and Regulatory Frameworks

**Charters and concessions**:
- **Granting authority**: King, parliament, local government
- **Duration**: Time-limited (though could be renewed)
- **Obligations**: Maintenance, repair standards
- **Rates**: Maximum tolls specified
- **Inspection**: Government monitors condition

**Rate regulation**:
- **Approval**: Government approves rate changes
- **Limits**: Maximum tolls
- **Complaints**: User can complain about rates, service
- **Dispute resolution**: Courts, government arbitration

**Standardized weights and measures**:
- **Vehicle classification**: How to classify different vehicles
- **Animal tolls**: Horses, carts, livestock
- **Pedestrian tolls**: Per person or unlimited for period

**Enforcement mechanisms**:
- **Toll collectors**: Authorized to collect
- **Law enforcement**: Support toll collectors
- **Courts**: Prosecute evasion, disputes

### D. Financial Analysis: Investment, Returns, and Risk

**Capital costs** (historical examples):

**Old London Bridge** (1209):
- **Cost**: Unknown (medieval records incomplete)
- **Funding**: Bridge Chapel rents, tolls, donations
- **Operation**: London Bridge Trust

**19th century American toll bridges**:
- **Typical construction cost**: $50,000-200,000 (large bridges)
- **Revenue**: $1,000-10,000 annually (depending on traffic)
- **Payback period**: 10-30 years (if successful)

**Stock company financing**:
- **Shares**: Sold to investors
- **Dividends**: Paid from toll revenue
- **Capital**: Raised through stock offerings

**Risk factors**:
- **Construction risk**: Cost overruns, delays
- **Revenue risk**: Lower than expected traffic
- **Competition**: Free bridges built nearby
- **Obsolescence**: Bridge destroyed, alternative routes
- **Political risk**: Government forbids tolls or municipalizes

**Case study: Brooklyn Bridge (built 1883, originally tolled)**:
- **Cost**: $15.5 million
- **Tolls**: 1-5 cents depending on vehicle type
- **Revenue**: Insufficient to cover construction + interest
- **Municipalization**: New York City purchased 1894, tolls removed

## III. Historical Case Studies: Famous Toll Bridges

### A. London Bridge: The Medieval Toll Bridge

**Old London Bridge** (1209-1831):

**Construction**:
- **Built**: Peter de Colechurch (1209)
- **Design**: Stone arch bridge, 19 arches
- **Features**: Buildings, chapel on bridge
- **Crowded**: Narrow, heavily used

**Tolling**:
- **Toll house**: At one end
- **Rates**: Varies by person, animal, cart
- **Exemptions**: Some people exempt (based on status)

**Bridge Chapel**:
- **Revenue**: Chapel rents supported bridge maintenance
- **Tolls**: Additional revenue source

**Funding and operation**:
- **Charitable donations**: Initial construction
- **Bridge estate**: Properties on bridge (houses, shops)
- **Tolls**: Ongoing maintenance

**Reconstruction**:
- **1831**: Demolished
- **Replacement**: New London Bridge (free access)

### B. American Toll Bridges: 19th Century Private Infrastructure

**Early American toll bridges**:

**Connecticut River toll bridges**:
- **Late 18th century**: Chartered companies build bridges
- **Financing**: Private investment, tolls
- **Economic role**: Connect communities, facilitate trade

**Covered bridges**:
- **Northeastern US**: New York, Vermont, New Hampshire
- **Purpose**: Protect wooden structure from weather
- **Tolls**: Recover costs

**Railroad era**:
- **Railroads**: Must pay tolls on bridges
- **Conflicts**: Railroads dislike toll costs
- **Volume discounts**: Negotiated by high-volume users

**Municipal acquisition**:
- **Late 19th century**: Cities and counties purchase
- **Bond issues**: Fund purchases
- **Tolls removed**: "Free bridges" become political goal

### C. The London Bridge Company: Corporate Toll Bridge Operation

**The Company** (1805-1920s):

**Formation**:
- **Creation**: Parliament acts consolidate bridge management
- **Authority**: London Bridge Company formed (1805)
- **Control**: Several bridges under single management

**Operations**:
- **Tolls**: Maintain tolls on bridges
- **Maintenance**: Keep bridges in repair
- **Traffic management**: Control flow

**Financial performance**:
- **Revenue**: Toll collection
- **Costs**: Maintenance, operations, staff
- **Profit**: Generally profitable

**Political pressure**:
- **Criticism**: Tolls seen as burden on commerce
- **Complaints**: Business community dislikes costs
- **Movement**: "Free London Bridge" campaigns

**Municipalization**:
- **1905**: London County Council purchases
- **1920s**: Bridge corporation abolished
- **Tolls removed**: Free access achieved

## IV. Social and Cultural Dimensions

### A. Equity and Access: The Price of Crossing

**Access barriers**:
- **Economic**: Toll costs exclude poorest users
- **Geographic**: Indirect routes add time, cost
- **Temporal**: Waiting for free times (if available)

**Class dimensions**:
- **Wealthy**: Can afford tolls, value time savings
- **Poor**: Cannot afford, must wait or use free alternatives
- **Business**: Regular users get volume discounts

**Social resentment**:
- **Perception**: Toll collectors seen as greedy
- **Resistance**: Toll evasion, protests
- **Politics**: Free bridge movements

### B. The "Free Bridge" Movement: Public Goods Philosophy

**19th century ideology**:
- **Infrastructure as public right**: Roads, bridges should be free
- **Taxation**: Should fund through general taxation
- **Equity**: Everyone regardless of ability to pay

**Political movements**:
- **Reformers**: Advocate municipal ownership
- **Labor movement**: Workers need affordable transportation
- **Business community**: Wants to reduce costs

**Government response**:
- **Municipalization**: Cities purchase toll bridges
- **Bond issues**: Fund purchases
- **Toll elimination**: Free access

**Philosophical shift**:
- **From commodity**: Bridge service as product sold
- **To public good**: Bridge access as right
- **Legacy**: Modern expectation of free infrastructure

### C. Bridge as Community: Social and Economic Hubs

**Toll bridge towns**:
- **Settlements**: Towns grow up at bridge approaches
- **Economic activity**: Inns, taverns, markets near tollhouses
- **Community identity**: Bridge shapes local identity

**Bridge as place**:
- **Public space**: Bridges used for socializing, commerce
- **Markets**: Vendors set up on bridge
- **Public events**: Festivals, celebrations

**Tollhouse as social space**:
- **Collection point**: Tollhouse as landmark
- **Gathering place**: People meet at tollhouse
- **Community hub**: Center of local activity

### D. Corruption, Evasion, and Enforcement

**Toll collector corruption**:
- **Pocketing**: Collectors keep some tolls
- **Underreporting**: Collect revenue underreported
- **Bribery**: Accepting bribes to lower tolls

**Evasion methods**:
- **Bypassing**: Swimming across river below bridge
- **Detour**: Take longer route to avoid toll
- **Forging**: False exemptions (clergy, local residents)
- **Violence**: Attacking toll collectors

**Enforcement challenges**:
- **Monitoring**: Difficult to track all crossings
- **Collection costs**: Enforcement reduces net revenue
- **Prosecution**: Legal action against evaders
- **Social pressure**: Community norms against evasion

**Technological solutions**:
- **Barriers**: Gates, barriers prevent bypassing
- **Recording**: Toll books, ledgers
- **Tokens**: Metal or wooden tokens (harder to counterfeit)
- **Surveillance**: Watchmen, guards

## V. Technological Displacement and the Rise of Free Infrastructure

### A. The Decline of Toll Bridges: Economic and Political Factors

**Economic factors**:
- **Alternative routes**: Increased bridge supply reduces toll value
- **Competition**: Free bridges divert traffic
- **Automobiles**: Need different infrastructure (wider, stronger)

**Political pressure**:
- **Free bridge movement**: Political parties adopt platform
- **Business pressure**: Merchants, industrialists want lower costs
- **Public opinion**: Tolls seen as burdens on commerce

**Technical changes**:
- **Larger spans**: Fewer bridges needed
- **Road networks**: Increased connectivity reduces bridge importance
- **Automobile age**: New infrastructure requirements

**Government financial capacity**:
- **Bond markets**: Municipalities can borrow for infrastructure
- **Taxation**: Income tax, property tax provide revenue
- **Economic growth**: Larger tax base

### B. Municipalization: The Transition to Public Ownership

**Acquisition process**:
- **Purchase**: Cities/taxing authorities buy toll bridges
- **Funding**: Municipal bonds, special assessments
- **Operation**: City takes over maintenance
- **Toll removal**: Access becomes free

**Financing municipalization**:
- **General obligation bonds**: Backed by taxing authority
- **Special assessments**: Property owners benefitting from bridge
- **Tax increases**: Slight tax increase for debt service

**Economic logic**:
- **Economies of scale**: Municipalities can borrow cheaper
- **Risk**: Government can spread risk across taxpayers
- **Efficiency**: Unified system cheaper than multiple tolls

**Timeline**:
- **United Kingdom**: Late 19th century - early 20th century
- **United States**: 1890s-1940s (accelerated during Great Depression)
- **Europe**: Gradual, 1900-1930s

### C. The Automobile Age: New Infrastructure, New Paradigm

**Automobile impact**:
- **Different requirements**: Wider, stronger bridges
- **Volume**: More traffic than horse-drawn era
- **Speed**: Higher speeds require safer infrastructure

**Highway construction**:
- **1930s-1950s**: Public works projects
- **Interstate Highway System** (US, 1956): Free highways
- **Free access**: Policy decision for free Interstate highways

**Urban planning**:
- **Bridge placement**: Highway planning, not toll collection
- **Design**: High-speed, free-flowing traffic
- **Financing**: Gas taxes, general taxation

**Exception retention**:
- **Tunnels**: Some tunnels retain tolls
-**Express lanes**: High-occupancy toll lanes (1940s, but expanded 1990s-2000s)
- **Bridge tolls**: Very rare (some large modern bridges have tolls)

### D. The Contemporary Resurgence: Congestion Pricing and Value Capture

**Philosophical shift**: From "cost recovery" to "demand management"

**Congestion pricing**:
- **Singapore**: Area Licensing Scheme (1975)
- **London**: Congestion Charge (2003)
- **Stockholm**: Congestion tax (2006)
- **Milan**: Ecopass (2012)

**Electronic toll collection**:
- **Transponders**: Electronic tags (E-ZPass, etc.)
- **License plate recognition**: Camera-based billing
- **Cashless**: No stopping to pay

**Value capture**:
- **Betterment taxes**: Capture increased property values near infrastructure
- **Special assessment**: Beneficiaries pay portion of costs
- **Joint development**: Airports, commercial development fund infrastructure

## VI. Contemporary Applications and Modern Parallels

### A. Modern Public-Private Partnerships: The Legacy of Toll Bridges

**PPP models**:
- **BOT (Build-Operate-Transfer)**: Private builds, operates for period, then transfers
- **BOOT (Build-Own-Operate-Transfer)**: Similar to BOT
- **DBFO**: Design-Build-Finance-Operate

**Toll bridge heritage**:

**Historical lessons**:
- **Risk**: Private investors bear construction risk

## VIII. Conclusion: From Commodity to Public Good

### A. Summary: The Rise and Fall of Toll Bridges

**Timeline**:
- **1100-1800**: Toll bridges common, infrastructure financing innovation
- **1800-1870**: Golden age (private toll bridge companies)
- **1870-1950**: Decline, municipalization, "free bridge" movement
- **1950-2000**: Free infrastructure dominant, tolls largely eliminated
- **2000-present**: Congestion pricing, electronic tolling, PPP revival

**What changed**:
- **Philosophy**: Infrastructure from commodity to public good
- **Financing**: From user fees to taxation
- **Access**: From restricted (toll-based) to universal (free)
- **Ownership**: From private (corporate) to public (governmental)

**What persists**:
- **Tolling concepts**: Congestion pricing, value capture
- **PPP models**: Private participation in infrastructure
- **User fees**: Some bridges, tunnels, roads retain tolls
- **Debates**: Questions about what should be free, what should cost

### B. Critical Assessment: The Commodification of Space and Movement

**Historical lessons**:
- **Innovation**: Toll bridges enabled infrastructure government couldn't fund
- **Efficiency**: Private capital more efficient than government (sometimes)
- **Equity**: Toll bridges created access barriers
- **Transition**: Shift from commodity to public good improved equity

**Contemporary relevance**:
- **Digital infrastructure**: Questions about access, tiered services
- **Transportation**: Congestion pricing, road pricing debates
- **PPP**: Private participation in infrastructure
- **Space**: Public space commodification (privatization debates)

**The price of "free"**:
- **Hidden costs**: "Free" infrastructure funded by taxes
- **Externalities**: "Free" access ignores congestion, pollution
- **Equity**: Tax-funded may be regressive (poor pay higher proportion)
- **Efficiency**: Free access can lead to overuse, congestion

**The future**:
- **Hybrid models**: Public-private partnerships, tolls for value capture
- **Technology**: Electronic tolling reduces costs, improves efficiency
- **Pricing**: Differentiated pricing (peak/off-peak, congestion pricing)
- **Debates**: Ongoing questions about what should be free, what should cost
