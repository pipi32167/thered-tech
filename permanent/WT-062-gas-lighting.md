# WT-062: Gas Lighting (Coal Gas Illumination)

## 🏛️ 技术名称和领域

**煤气照明** - 照明技术/能源系统/城市基础设施

## 📜 简史

1790年代威廉·默多克（William Murdoch）发明，通过加热煤炭产生可燃气体，通过管道输送到灯具燃烧发光。1810年代伦敦开始建立煤气公司，19世纪中期成为欧美城市主要照明方式。工厂、街道、商店、住宅纷纷采用煤气灯，彻底改变了人类夜生活和工作模式。1880年代爱迪生电灯出现后开始衰落，但煤气灯延续使用至20世纪中叶。1950-60年代基本消失，被电力照明彻底取代。

## ⚰️ 枯萎原因

电灯更安全（无煤气泄漏、爆炸风险）、更清洁（无燃烧产物）、更方便（开关即亮、无需调节火焰）。煤气照明需要本地煤气厂、管道网络基础设施、持续维护。爱迪生中心电站模式（1882年）证明电力集中供应可行。白炽灯泡效率更高、更明亮。20世纪城市电气化加速，煤气灯因安全风险、维护成本被淘汰。1950-60年代最后一批街道煤气灯被电灯取代。

## 💡 当代启发

首个**大规模市政能源网络**，建立了通过管道输送能源的集中供应模式，预见了电气化和天然气网络。煤气照明催生了**公用事业公司**商业模式，按使用量计费、基础设施投资、网络化服务。作为首个将**黑夜变为白天**的技术，深刻改变了人类时间感、工作模式、城市生活节奏。煤气灯的**化学储能**概念（煤炭→气体→能量）与现代能源转换思路相通。在能源转型背景下，煤气照明的技术锁定和基础设施惯性对当代**可再生能源转型**有启示：既有能源系统如何被新系统取代。

# Gas Lighting (Coal Gas Illumination)

## Technical Overview

Gas lighting represented humanity's first major step beyond direct combustion for artificial lighting. By producing fuel gas from coal and distributing it through pipes to burn at point of use, gas lighting created the first centralized energy distribution network and fundamentally transformed urban life in the 19th century.

### Production: Coal Gas Manufacture

**Coal gasification process**:

**Retort heating**:
- Coal placed in sealed retorts (oven-like chambers)
- Heated to temperatures of 1000-1300°C
- Absence of air prevents combustion
- Heat applied externally (originally from coal fires, later from waste gas)

**Volatile release**:
- Coal heated in absence of air undergoes destructive distillation
- Volatile components vaporize
- Major products:
  - **Hydrogen** (H₂): 50-55% by volume
  - **Methane** (CH₄): 25-35%
  - **Carbon monoxide** (CO): 8-12%
  - **Carbon dioxide** (CO₂): 2-4%
  - **Nitrogen** (N₂): 3-8%
  - Hydrocarbons (ethane, ethylene, etc.): 2-5%

**Tar and ammonia byproducts**:
- Coal tar condensed and collected
- Ammonia liquor washed from gas
- Both valuable chemical products
- Helped make gasification economically viable

**Purification**:
- Gas passed through scrubbers
- Removal of impurities:
  - Sulfur compounds (corrosive, foul-smelling)
  - Naphthalene (would condense in pipes)
  - Ammonia
- Purified gas had higher heating value and burned cleaner

**Storage and distribution**:
- Gas stored in gas holders (telescopic bell tanks)
- Maintained constant pressure
- Distributed through cast iron pipe networks
- Pressure regulated for different applications

**Efficiency considerations**:
- Energy conversion efficiency: 60-80% (coal energy to gas energy)
- Thermal efficiency of gas lamps: 1-3% (gas energy to visible light)
- Overall efficiency: Very low by modern standards
- But superior to candles and oil lamps of the time

### Distribution Infrastructure

**Gas holder design**:
- Telescoping cylindrical tanks
- Open at bottom, sealed water reservoir
- Gas lifted tank as volume increased
- Maintained constant pressure through weight
- Sizes ranged from small to enormous (millions of cubic feet capacity)

**Pipe networks**:
- **Mains**: Large pipes (6-12 inches diameter) from gas works
- **Services**: Smaller pipes to individual buildings
- **Meters**: Measured consumption for billing
- **Pressure regulation**: Reduced pressure for street vs. domestic use

**Material evolution**:
- Early: Wood pipes (short-lived, leaky)
- 1810s onward: Cast iron (durable, standardized)
- Jointing: Lead caulked joints, later threaded connections
- By late 19th century: Some wrought steel and wrought iron

**Coverage expansion**:
- Initially: City centers, wealthy districts
- Expansion: Middle-class residential areas
- Eventually: Working-class districts (slower due to cost)
- Urban concentration: Rural areas generally not served

**Maintenance challenges**:
- Leaks: Explosion hazard, waste
- Blockages: Tar condensation, naphthalene deposits
- Corrosion: Gas impurities attacked metal
- Tree roots: Damaged underground pipes

### Lighting Fixtures

**Street lighting**:
- **Gas lamps** with multiple mantles or burners
- Glass globes to protect flame and direct light
- Clockwork or hydraulic mechanisms for automatic ignition
- Reflectors to direct light downward
- Typically mounted on cast iron posts

**Interior lighting**:
- **Gas brackets** (wall-mounted fixtures)
- **Gas chandeliers** (ceiling-mounted, multiple flames)
- **Portable gas lamps** (connected by flexible hose)
- Dimming through gas flow control

**Burner types**:
- **Argand burner**: Circular wick, glass chimney, improved airflow
- **Fish-tail burner**: Slotted burner, produced fishtail-shaped flame
- **Regenerative burner**: Preheated gas and air, improved efficiency
- **Incandescent mantle** (late 19th century): Rare earth oxides, much brighter

**Lighting characteristics**:
- Color temperature: Warm yellow-orange (~2000K)
- Brightness: 10-100 candlepower depending on size
- Flicker: Present but less than candles or oil lamps
- Heat output: Substantial (most energy became heat, not light)

**Safety features**:
- Excess flow valves: Prevented dangerous pressure buildup
- Flame failure devices: Shut off gas if flame extinguished
- Ventilation requirements: Prevented carbon monoxide accumulation
- Glass chimneys: Protected flame, directed airflow

### Efficiency and Performance

**Luminous efficacy**:
- Early burners: 0.1-0.2 lumens per watt
- Argand burners: 0.3-0.5 lumens per watt
- Incandescent mantles: 2-5 lumens per watt
- Comparison:
  - Candles: 0.1-0.2 lumens per watt
  - Early incandescent lamps: 1-4 lumens per watt
  - Modern LEDs: 80-150 lumens per watt

**Brightness comparison**:
- Single gas flame: ~10 candlepower
- Multi-flame street lamp: 100-500 candlepower
- Gas lamp with mantle: 200-1000 candlepower
- Early electric lamp (1880s): 16-50 candlepower
- Modern household lamp: 800+ candlepower

**Operating characteristics**:
- Instant on/off (once lit)
- Adjustable brightness
- Required regular maintenance:
  - Cleaning glass chimneys
  - Trimming or replacing burners
  - Replacing mantles (when used)
  - Checking for leaks

**Cost considerations**:
- Initial infrastructure expensive
- Operating costs moderate
- Competition from electricity initially more expensive
- Gas lighting cost advantage eroded over time

## Brief History

### Early Development (1790s-1810s)

**William Murdoch's experiments**:
- Scottish engineer working for Boulton & Watt
- Began experiments 1790s
- First lit his house in Redruth, Cornwall (1792)
- Used gas to light Boulton & Watt's Soho factory (1798)
- Demonstrated to potential investors and clients

**Technical challenges**:
- Producing gas consistently
- Purifying foul-smelling impurities
- Preventing tar condensation in pipes
- Maintaining pressure
- Designing reliable burners

**First practical installations**:
- 1802: Soho Foundry lit by gas
- 1805: Cotton mill in Manchester
- 1807: Street lighting demonstration in London (Pall Mall)
- Proved viability for industrial and street lighting

### Commercialization (1810-1830s)

**First gas companies**:
- **Gas Light and Coke Company** (London, 1812): First commercial gas company
- Charter granted to supply gas to London
- Initially served wealthy districts and commercial areas
- Model for subsequent gas companies worldwide

**Rapid expansion**:
- London: Multiple gas companies competing
- Other British cities: Glasgow, Manchester, Liverpool, etc.
- International spread: Paris, Berlin, Baltimore, Boston
- Each city required local gas works construction

**Industrial applications**:
- Factories: Extended working hours
- Mills: Improved productivity
- Workshops: Better visibility for precision work
- Economic benefits justified infrastructure investment

**Street lighting**:
- City councils contracted with gas companies
- Improved public safety
- Extended commercial and social hours
- Symbol of civic progress and modernity

**Residential adoption**:
- Initially: Wealthy households
- Gradual: Middle-class adoption
- Slow: Working-class adoption (cost barrier)
- By mid-century: Common in urban middle-class homes

### Golden Age (1840s-1880s)

**Infrastructure maturation**:
- Comprehensive urban gas networks
- Improved purification and distribution
- Larger gas works
- Standardized equipment

**Technological improvements**:
- **Incandescent mantle** (Carl Auer von Welsbach, 1880s): Dramatic brightness increase
- **Regenerative burners**: Improved efficiency
- **Automatic lighting systems**: Timer and clockwork ignition
- **Metering**: Accurate consumption measurement

**Applications expanded**:
- **Street lighting**: Extensive urban networks
- **Commercial**: Shops, offices, theaters, restaurants
- **Industrial**: Factories, mills, workshops
- **Residential**: Middle and upper-class homes
- **Institutional**: Hospitals, schools, government buildings

**Economic importance**:
- Major urban utility
- Significant employer
- Investment vehicle
- Tax revenue source

**Social impact**:
- Extended productive hours
- Enabled evening entertainment and socializing
- Improved public safety
- Changed urban character

**Competition emerges**:
- **Electric lighting** (1879 onward): Initially expensive and unreliable
- Early competition limited to prestige applications
- Gas lighting remained dominant through 1880s

### Competition and Decline (1880s-1920s)

**Edison's electric light** (1879):
- Demonstrated centralized electric generation
- Pearl Street Station (New York, 1882): First central power plant
- Initially more expensive than gas
- But advantages: cleaner, no fumes, instant bright light, no heat

**Improving electric technology**:
- More efficient lamps
- Better generators
- Improved distribution
- Declining costs

**Gas lighting response**:
- Incandescent mantles: Dramatic efficiency and brightness improvement
- Reduced prices
- Improved service
- But couldn't match electricity's convenience and cleanliness

**Market segmentation**:
- Electricity captured high-end applications
- Gas maintained price advantage in some markets
- Rural areas: Gas too expensive, electricity also limited
- Competition was regional and application-specific

**World War I effects**:
- Disrupted development of both systems
- Post-war: Accelerated electrical adoption
- Investment shifted to electricity
- Gas lighting seen as old-fashioned

**Declining market share**:
- Street lighting: Gradual conversion to electric
- Residential: New construction electrical
- Commercial: Preference for electric
- Industrial: Conversion to electric for most applications

### Final Phase (1920s-1960s)

**Niche persistence**:
- Some cities maintained gas street lighting into mid-20th century
- Cost considerations in some applications
- Institutional inertia and existing infrastructure
- Aesthetic preferences (historical districts)

**Complete replacement**:
- Street lighting: Last cities converted in 1950s-1960s
- Residential: Virtually disappeared
- Industrial: Rare exceptions
- Gas supply shifted to heating and cooking

**Natural gas transition**:
- Gas production shifted from manufactured to natural gas
- Pipeline networks repurposed
- Distribution infrastructure maintained but for different uses
- Gas remained important as fuel but not for lighting

**Cultural memory**:
- Nostalgia for gas lamp era
- Preservation of historic gas lamps
- Museum pieces and cultural artifacts
- Symbol of Victorian era

## Why It Withered

### Fundamental Efficiency Limitations

**Energy conversion inefficiency**:
- Coal to gas: 60-80% efficient
- Gas to light: 1-5% efficient (even with mantles)
- Overall efficiency: <4%
- Electric lighting: Eventually 10-20% efficient (modern LEDs much higher)

**Heat waste**:
- 95%+ of energy became heat rather than light
- Made spaces uncomfortably warm in summer
- Ventilation required, wasting more energy
- Electric lights produced less waste heat

**Light quality limitations**:
- Warm yellow color limited color rendering
- Flicker present (though reduced with mantles)
- Dim by modern standards
- Required multiple lamps for adequate room lighting

### Safety and Health Issues

**Combustion hazards**:
- **Gas leaks**: Explosion and fire risk
- **Incomplete combustion**: Carbon monoxide poisoning
- **Burn risk**: Open flames dangerous
- **Fire hazard**: Flames ignited nearby materials

**Health effects**:
- **Indoor air pollution**: Combustion byproducts
- **Carbon monoxide**: Odorless, deadly
- **Soot and smoke**: Respiratory irritation
- **Oxygen depletion**: In poorly ventilated spaces

**Installation risks**:
- Pipe leaks in buildings
- Accumulation in confined spaces
- Explosion hazard from accumulated gas
- Maintenance required trained technicians

**Public safety concerns**:
- Street lamps: Fire hazard to nearby structures
- Leaks: Could cause explosions
- Required constant vigilance and maintenance

**Electrical advantages**:
- No combustion products
- No explosion risk from leaks
- No open flames
- Safer in all applications

### Infrastructure and Maintenance Burdens

**Capital intensive**:
- Gas works construction: Expensive
- Pipe network installation: Costly
- Ongoing maintenance: Continuous expense
- Upgrades: Required periodic reinvestment

**Maintenance requirements**:
- Lamp lighting and extinguishing (manual labor intensive)
- Cleaning: Glass chimneys required regular cleaning
- Burner replacement: Worn parts needed replacement
- Mantle replacement: Fragile, frequent replacement
- Leak detection and repair: Constant vigilance

**Labor costs**:
- Lamp lighters: Street lamps required manual operation
- Maintenance crews: Continuous maintenance required
- Meter readers: Consumption measurement
- Emergency response: Leaks and outages required immediate attention

**Infrastructure lock-in**:
- Once gas network built, expensive to abandon
- Transition required new infrastructure investment
- Dual systems (gas and electric) expensive to maintain
- Economic pressure to delay conversion

**Electrical advantages**:
- Minimal per-point maintenance
- No fuel distribution at point of use
- Automated switching possible
- Lower long-term operating costs

### Technological Superiority of Electricity

**Brightness and control**:
- Electric lamps: Brighter instantly
- Dimming: Possible with simple controls
- No flame adjustment required
- Multiple brightness levels easily achieved

**Cleanliness**:
- No combustion products
- No soot or smoke
- No odor
- Cleanlier indoor environments

**Convenience**:
- Switch operation: Simple and instantaneous
- No lighting or extinguishing required
- No fuel delivery at point of use
- Wires less bulky than gas pipes

**Versatility**:
- Electric lamps: Many shapes and sizes
- Flexible placement: Wires easier to route than pipes
- Portable devices: Possible with batteries or extension cords
- Gas limited by pipe network

**Scalability**:
- Electrical generation: Large plants very efficient
- Transmission: Electricity can travel long distances
- Urban density: Wires easier to install in dense cities than additional gas infrastructure

### Economic Factors

**Cost trends**:
- **Electricity**: Declining costs throughout 20th century
- **Gas lighting**: Costs declined slowly, then stabilized
- **Crossover point**: Electricity became cheaper in 1910s-1920s for many applications
- **Maintenance**: Electric lighting lower long-term costs

**Investment shifts**:
- Utilities invested in electrical generation
- Gas companies diversified into natural gas, heating
- New construction: Electrical as standard
- Retrofits: Gradual conversion to electric

**Regulatory factors**:
- Safety regulations increased gas lighting costs
- Building codes favored electricity
- Utility regulation favored larger electrical systems
- Government electrification initiatives

**Market dynamics**:
- Network effects: Electrical adoption encouraged more adoption
- Social preference: Electric seen as modern
- Professional advice: Architects, engineers recommended electric
- Consumer preference: Convenience, cleanliness

### Cumulative Disadvantage and Transition

**Multiple reinforcing factors**:
- Efficiency: Electric much more efficient
- Safety: Electric much safer
- Convenience: Electric much more convenient
- Cost: Electric became cheaper
- Cleanliness: Electric much cleaner
- Brightness: Electric much brighter

**Transition pattern**:
- No single fatal flaw
- Accumulation of disadvantages
- Electric advantages overwhelming
- Niche persistence despite overall inferiority
- Eventual complete replacement

**Path dependence**:
- Existing gas infrastructure delayed conversion
- Sunk costs in gas network
- But eventual conversion inevitable
- Transition cost justified by long-term benefits

**Geographic variation**:
- Wealthy cities converted first
- Industrialized nations: Earlier conversion
- Rural areas: Different dynamics (neither gas nor electric initially)
- Developing world: Sometimes skipped gas lighting entirely, went directly to electric

## Contemporary Insights

### Energy Infrastructure and Network Effects

Gas lighting established patterns for energy infrastructure that persist:

**Centralized production model**:
- Fuel converted to useful form at central facility
- Distribution to multiple consumers
- Economies of scale in production
- Model later replicated by electrical generation

**Network effects**:
- Value increases with more users
- Infrastructure costs spread across larger user base
- New users reduce per-unit costs
- Creates natural monopoly tendencies

**Utility business model**:
- Continuous supply commodity
- Metered billing based on usage
- Infrastructure investment recouped over long periods
- Regulatory framework for natural monopolies

**Lock-in effects**:
- Once infrastructure built, difficult to replace
- Sunk costs create resistance to change
- New competitors must build parallel infrastructure
- Creates market power for incumbent utilities

**Contemporary parallels**:
- Electrical grids evolved from gas distribution patterns
- Internet service follows similar utility model
- Renewable energy faces similar infrastructure challenges
- Transition costs from fossil fuel infrastructure

### Technological Transitions and System Inertia

Gas lighting to electric lighting illustrates technological transition dynamics:

**Gradual transition**:
- Not immediate replacement
- Decades of coexistence
- Different applications converted at different rates
- Street lighting: Slowest (infrastructure investments)
- New construction: Fastest (no sunk costs)

**Sunk cost effects**:
- Existing gas infrastructure created disincentive to convert
- Cities already invested in gas networks
- Conversion meant abandoning investment
- Delayed adoption of superior technology

**Infrastructure coexistence**:
- Some cities maintained both systems for decades
- Gradual conversion of neighborhoods
- Hybrid systems in transition period
- Maintenance of both expensive

**Generational effects**:
- Earlier adopters: Professional and wealthy classes
- Later adopters: More conservative users
- Institutional inertia: Bureaucracies slow to change
- Leadership in transition: Some cities led, others followed

**Contemporary relevance**:
- Similar patterns in renewable energy transition
- Fossil fuel infrastructure creates lock-in
- Transition costs and stranded assets
- Policy challenges in accelerating transitions

Gas lighting illustrates efficiency evolution:

**Conservation implications**:
- Historical lighting was expensive and inefficient

**Lesson**:
