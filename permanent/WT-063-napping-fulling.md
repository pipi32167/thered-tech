# WT-063: Napping (Fulling and Teazle Processing)

## 🏛️ 技术名称和领域

**起绒/缩绒工艺** - 纺织后整理/表面处理技术

## 📜 简史

起源于新石器时代，通过毡化原理使羊毛织物收缩增厚。中世纪发展为使用**起绒机**和**刺果起绒**（teazle/thistle heads）在织物表面拉出绒毛。16-18世纪英格兰西部产绒区（West of England）将此工艺推向巅峰，创造出高贵华丽的粗剪绒、天鹅绒和植绒织物。工业革命后起绒机械化，但仍依赖刺果手工操作。19世纪末人造纤维和针织技术出现后逐渐衰落，20世纪基本消失，仅保留在高端奢侈品制造中。

## ⚰️ 枯萎原因

针织技术和人造纤维提供了更廉价、更简单的绒面织物生产方法。针织物天然有弹性，更容易起绒。人造纤维（尼龙、聚酯）可以直接纺出绒面纱线。刺果起绒需要大量手工操作，效率低且成本高。合成绒面织物（如人造毛皮）更便宜、更耐用。纺织工业向大规模工业化发展，传统后整理工艺逐渐消失。1970-80年代最后一批传统起绒工坊关闭。

## 💡 当代启发

代表了**材料表面工程**的早期实践，通过物理方法改变材料表面性能而不改变本体。刺果起绒的**生物工具**概念——利用自然结构的精准特性——预见了现代**仿生学**思路。起绒工艺的"层次构建"（添加表面纤维层）与现代**复合材料**、**3D打印**的表面纹理化相通。作为支撑欧洲纺织业数百年的精细工艺，起绒技术体现了传统制造中对**触觉品质**的追求，在数字时代提醒我们**物质性**和**触感体验**的价值。高端纺织品制造中仍有有限应用，证明传统工艺在奢侈品领域的持久价值。

# Napping (Fulling and Teazle Processing)

## Technical Overview

Napping and fulling represent ancient textile finishing techniques that transform loose-woven wool fabrics into dense, warm, often fuzzy materials through controlled felting and surface fiber manipulation. These processes were essential for creating everything from sturdy woolen cloth to luxurious velvet fabrics, and they remained crucial textile technologies for millennia before industrialization and synthetic fibers rendered them largely obsolete.

### Fundamental Principles

**Fulling (缩绒/毡化)**:
- **Felting mechanism**: Wool fibers have microscopic scales that interlock when subjected to moisture, heat, and mechanical agitation
- **Fabric shrinkage**: Fabric shrinks 20-50% in both dimensions
- **Density increase**: Loose weave becomes dense, tight fabric
- **Thickening**: Thin cloth becomes substantial, warm material
- **Water and soap**: Fuller's earth (clay) or soap aided cleaning and felting
- **Mechanical action**: Originally by foot (walking on wet cloth), later by water-powered or steam-powered fulling mills

**Napping (起绒)**:
- **Fiber extraction**: Pull fibers from fabric body to create surface pile
- **Tool evolution**:
  - **Thistles and teasels**: Natural burr-like seed heads used to comb fibers
  - **Wire cards**: Metal brushes with fine wires
  - **Teazle frames**: Frames holding dried teasel heads
  - **Gigging machines**: Rotating cylinders covered with teasels or wire cards
- **Directionality**: Typically raised nap in one direction for uniform appearance
- **Surface modification**: Creates insulating air pockets, softer hand feel, different appearance

**Teazle (刺果) technology**:
- **Botanical source**: *Dipsacus fullonum* (fuller's teasel), cultivated specifically for napping
- **Natural hooks**: Modified bracts act as tiny, consistent hooks
- **Uniformity**: Each teasel has hundreds of identically sized hooks
- **Superior to metal**: Natural hooks more gentle, less likely to damage fabric
- **Cultivation**: Major agricultural crop in textile-producing regions
- **Harvest and processing**: Dried, mounted on frames, used until worn

### Historical Development

**Ancient origins (Neolithic - Classical Antiquity)**:
- **Felting discovered**: Likely accidental discovery through wet wool exposure
- **Early fulling**: By foot-stomping in troughs or streams
- **Fuller's earth**: Clay used as cleaning agent and felting aid
- **Roman period**: Professional fullers (fullones) as important trade
- **Pompeii evidence**: Fulling workshops excavated with fulling vats

**Medieval development (500-1500 CE)**:
- **Water-powered fulling mills**: 12th-13th centuries, water wheels operated trip hammers
- **Fulling mills**: Important technological development in textile processing
- **Regional specialization**: Different regions specialized in different types of fulled cloth
- **Guild control**: Fullers' guilds regulated quality and practices

**Early Modern period (1500-1800)**:
- **Teazle cultivation**: Systematic cultivation of Dipsacus fullonum
- **West of England cloth**: Famous high-quality broadcloth requiring extensive fulling and napping
- **Velvet production**: Complex napping processes for luxury fabrics
- **Raising techniques**: Multiple raising and shearing cycles for quality control

**Industrial Revolution (1750-1850)**:
- **Mechanical napping**: Gigging machines with teasel-covered cylinders
- **Steam power**: Replaced water power for fulling mills
- **Chemical aids**: Improved soaps and detergents
- **Quality control**: Standardization of finishes

**Peak and decline (1850-1950)**:
- **Competition from knitting**: Knit fabrics easier to nap and more flexible
- **Synthetic fibers**: Nylon, polyester don't felt, don't nap traditionally
- **Cost pressures**: Labor-intensive processes increasingly expensive
- **Niche persistence**: High-end woolens, some luxury fabrics

### Manufacturing Processes

**Fulling process**:

1. **Cloth preparation**:
   - Woven woolen cloth, often loosely woven
   - woven from "woolen" preparation (carded, not combed wool)
   - Contains natural oils (lanolin) and impurities

2. **Fulling vats**:
   - Large vats filled with warm water
   - Fuller's earth (clay) or soap added
   - Cloth submerged and agitated

3. **Mechanical action**:
   - **Traditional**: Foot fulling (walking on cloth)
   - **Medieval**: Water-powered fulling stocks (wooden hammers raised by cam)
   - **Industrial**: Steam-powered fulling stocks

4. **Agitation cycle**:
   - Cloth pounded and agitated for hours
   - Heat maintained (warm water)
   - Fuller's earth absorbed oils and dirt
   - Periodic rinsing and re-application

5. **Shrinkage and densification**:
   - Fabric shrank substantially
   - Loose weave became tight
   - Thickness increased dramatically
   - Water-resistant quality developed

6. **Drying and stretching**:
   - Cloth stretched on tenter frames to prevent uneven shrinkage
   - Dried under tension
   - Tenter hooks (permanent marks on cloth edges)

7. **Raising** (napping often followed fulling):
   - Surface fibers raised to create nap
   - Multiple cycles of raising and shearing
   - Created velvety or fuzzy surface

**Napping process**:

1. **Teazle preparation**:
   - Cultivation of Dipsacus fullonum
   - Harvested when mature
   - Dried thoroughly
   - Mounted on wooden frames or cylinders

2. **Gigging (machine napping)**:
   - Fabric passed over rotating cylinders covered with teasels
   - Directional: Always nap in same direction for uniformity
   - Multiple passes: Gradual fiber extraction
   - Tension control: Critical for uniform results

3. **Shearing**:
   - After napping, surface often uneven
   - Cropping shears used to level nap
   - Skilled shearers ("croppers") specialized in this work
   - Multiple napping/shearing cycles for quality fabrics

4. **Singeing** (sometimes):
   - Passing fabric over flame to burn off loose fibers
   - Created smooth surface
   - Required skill to avoid damage

5. **Final finishing**:
   - Pressing for smoothness
   - Steaming for luster
   - Calendering for surface effects

**Velvet production** (specialized napping):

**Woven velvet** (more complex):
- Double cloth woven with interweaving
- Slit between layers to create pile
- Pile cut to uniform height
- Origins in medieval period, highly developed in Renaissance

**Union velvet** (simpler):
- Plain weave base with extra weft threads forming loops
- Loops cut after weaving to create pile
- Less expensive than woven velvet
- Required specialized looms

**Processing**:
- Weaving with extra threads
- Loop formation during weaving
- Cutting operations (knife or wire)
- Pile shearing for uniform height
- Multiple finishing steps

### Technical Characteristics

**Fabric properties**:

**Before fulling**:
- Loose weave
- Open structure
- Relatively transparent
- Weak and prone to distortion
- Rough hand feel

**After fulling**:
- Dense, tight structure
- Opaque
- Strong and durable
- Warm (insulating)
- Water-resistant (wool's natural oils plus density)
- Substantial hand feel

**After napping**:
- Fuzzy or hairy surface
- Enhanced insulation (air trapped in nap)
- Soft hand feel
- Different visual appearance (light interaction changes)
- Can be sheared to various heights

**Quality factors**:
- **Uniformity**: Consistent nap length crucial
- **Directionality**: All nap should lie in same direction
- **Density**: Appropriate fiber density per area
- **Fiber quality**: Long, strong fibers produce better nap
- **Base fabric**: Must be strong enough to withstand napping

**Applications**:

**Fulling primarily**:
- Woolen broadcloth: Standard fabric for clothing
- Blankets: Warm, dense fabric
- Coatings: Heavy, weather-resistant outerwear
- Military cloth: Durable uniforms and equipment

**Napping primarily**:
- Flannel: Soft, fuzzy fabric for clothing and bedding
- Broadcloth: Smooth but napped surface
- Velvet: Luxury fabric with cut pile
- Plush: Similar to velvet but longer pile
- Union velvet: Less expensive velvet alternative

## Brief History

### Ancient Origins to Classical Antiquity

**Discovery of felting**:
- **Accidental discovery**: Probably occurred when woven wool got wet and agitated
- **Observation**: Noticed that agitation caused shrinkage and thickening
- **Intentional use**: Deliberate fulling by at least Bronze Age
- **Archaeological evidence**: Fulling vats at archaeological sites

**Roman fullers**:
- **Professional trade**: Fullers (fullones) established profession
- **Workshops**: Fullonicae found in Roman cities (Pompeii, Herculaneum)
- **Process**: Used fuller's earth, urine (ammonia source), and water
- **Social role**: Cleaning and finishing cloth, important urban industry

**Technology**:
- **Fulling vats**: Large basins for wet processing
- **Fulling by foot**: Workers walked on cloth in vats
- **Drying**: Cloth stretched and dried
- **Pressing**: Heavy stones or presses for smoothing

### Medieval Period (500-1500 CE)

**Water-powered fulling mills**:
- **Innovation**: 12th-13th centuries in Europe
- **Mechanical action**: Water wheels raised cam-operated trip hammers
- **Location**: Streams and rivers with reliable flow
- **Economic impact**: Made fulling more efficient, reduced labor

**Regional specialization**:
- **England**: West of England broadcloth famous
- **Flanders**: Fine woolens
- **Italy**: Luxury fabrics, including early velvets
- **Each region**: Developed distinctive cloths and techniques

**Teazle cultivation**:
- **Domestication**: Dipsacus fullonum cultivated
- **Agriculture**: Important crop in textile regions
- **Harvesting**: Careful timing for optimal hook development
- **Processing**: Drying and preparation for use

**Guild regulation**:
- **Quality control**: Guilds maintained standards
- **Trade secrets**: Techniques protected
- **Training**: Apprenticeships for skill transmission
- **Professional identity**: Fullers as distinct craft

### Early Modern Period (1500-1800)

**West of England broadcloth**:
- **Famous cloth**: High-quality woolen broadcloth
- **Extensive fulling**: Heavy shrinkage for density
- **Multiple napping**: Several raising and shearing cycles
- **Premium product**: Exported throughout Europe

**Velvet development**:
- **Origins**: 13th-14th centuries (possibly earlier in China)
- **Renaissance perfection**: Italian velvets particularly prized
- **Woven velvet**: Complex double-cloth weaving
- **Union velvet**: Simpler loop-weaving technique

**Technological refinements**:
- **Improved teasel frames**: Better control of napping
- **Shearing techniques**: Specialized croppers
- **Quality control**: Multiple inspections and finishing steps
- **Chemical aids**: Better soaps and processing agents

**Agricultural integration**:
- **Teazle farming**: Specialized cultivation
- **Sheep breeding**: Wool bred for specific felting qualities
- **Fuller's earth**: Mined and processed for fulling

### Industrial Revolution (1750-1850)

**Mechanization**:
- **Fulling mills**: Water-powered, then steam-powered
- **Gigging machines**: Rotating cylinders with teasels or wire cards
- **Continuous processing**: Improved efficiency
- **Standardization**: More uniform results

**Steam power**:
- **Reliable power**: Steam engines provided consistent power
- **Location independence**: No longer tied to water power
- **Larger facilities**: Bigger mills and factories
- **Centralization**: Production concentrated in larger facilities

**Chemical improvements**:
- **Detergents**: Better cleaning agents than fuller's earth
- **Processing aids**: Chemicals to improve felting and napping
- **Quality**: More consistent, controllable processes

**Scale and efficiency**:
- **Increased production**: Larger quantities, faster processing
- **Lower costs**: Economies of scale
- **Broader markets**: More people could afford fulled woolens
- **Standardization**: More uniform products

### 19th Century Peak and Competition

**Peak production**:
- **Early 19th century**: Traditional processes at peak
- **Regional dominance**: West of England, Flanders, etc.
- **Quality reputation**: Specific regions famous for quality

**Competitive challenges**:
- **Knitted fabrics**: Often easier and cheaper to produce
- **Cotton competition**: Cotton fabrics cheaper for some uses
- **Wool cost**: Wool increasingly expensive compared to cotton

**Niche persistence**:
- **High-end woolens**: Luxury and quality markets
- **Traditional preferences**: Some consumers preferred traditional woolens
- **Specialized applications**: Military, uniforms, formal wear

### 20th Century Decline

**Synthetic fibers**:
- **Nylon (1930s)**: Don't felt, don't nap traditionally
- **Polyester**: Different surface characteristics
- **Blends**: Wool blends sometimes still napped
- **Cost advantages**: Synthetics cheaper and more consistent

**Industrial changes**:
- **Labor costs**: Hand napping increasingly expensive
- **Mechanical limitations**: Some tasks still required hand work
- **Cost pressure**: Global competition, cost reduction pressures

**Knitted fabric advantages**:
- **Natural stretch**: More comfortable
- **Easier napping**: Loop structure easier to work with
- **Lower cost**: Often cheaper to produce
- **Faster production**: Less processing time

**Consumer preferences**:
- **Casualization**: Less formal clothing
- **Care requirements**: Woolens require careful care
- **Cost**: Traditional fulled woolens expensive
- **Synthetics**: Easier care, cheaper

**Final decline**:
- **1930s-1950s**: Rapid decline in traditional fulling and napping
- **1960s-1970s**: Most traditional facilities closed
- **1980s-1990s**: Only limited luxury production
- **21st century**: Small-scale revival in luxury/heritage sectors

## Why It Withered

### Fundamental Material Limitations

**Wool-specific technology**:
- **Requires wool**: Only animal fibers with scales felt effectively
- **Fiber properties**: Scales on wool fibers essential for felting
- **Plant fibers**: Cotton, linen don't felt (no scales)
- **Synthetic fibers**: Don't have surface structure for felting

**Material constraints**:
- **Cost**: Wool increasingly expensive relative to alternatives
- **Availability**: Wool production limited by agricultural factors
- **Quality variability**: Natural fibers vary in quality
- **Processing requirements**: Wool requires careful handling

**Alternative fabrics**:
- **Cotton**: Cheaper, easier to care for, don't require fulling
- **Knitted wool**: Easier to process, more flexible
- **Synthetic fibers**: Different properties, don't require traditional finishing

### Process Complexity and Labor Intensity

**Multi-step processes**:
- **Fulling**: Preparation, fulling, drying, stretching, raising, shearing
- **Napping**: Multiple cycles for quality products
- **Quality control**: Each step required skill and oversight
- **Time**: Days to process cloth from weaving to finishing

**Skilled labor requirements**:
- **Fullers**: Knowledgeable about felting chemistry and timing
- **Croppers**: Skill at shearing nap to uniform height
- **Teazle preparers**: Knowledge of teasel cultivation and preparation
- **Quality inspectors**: Experience evaluating finished cloth

**Hand labor intensity**:
- **Some tasks resisted mechanization**: Final quality control, certain shearing operations
- **Teazle replacement**: Metal teasels (cards) available but inferior
- **Craft skills**: Certain operations required experienced judgment
- **Cost**: Skilled hand labor increasingly expensive

**Infrastructure requirements**:
- **Fulling mills**: Specialized equipment
- **Water power**: Originally required, later steam
- **Drying facilities**: Large areas for stretching and drying cloth
- **Teazel cultivation**: Agricultural land and expertise

### Competition from Alternative Technologies

**Knitted fabrics**:
- **Easier production**: Often simpler than woven and fulled cloth
- **Natural stretch**: More comfortable, flexible
- **Easier napping**: Loop structure more amenable to raising nap
- **Lower cost**: Often less expensive to produce

**Synthetic pile fabrics**:
- **Artificial velvet**: Often cheaper than woven velvet
- **Faux fur**: Synthetic pile fabrics
- **Performance**: Often more durable than natural pile fabrics
- **Consistency**: More uniform quality

**Engineered surface treatments**:
- **Modern finishing**: Modern chemical and mechanical finishing
- **Singeing**: Burn-off of loose fibers
- **Calendering**: Surface compression and smoothing
- **Chemical treatments**: Modern surface modification

### Economic and Market Factors

**Cost pressures**:
- **Labor costs**: Traditional processes labor-intensive
- **Raw material costs**: Wool expensive relative to alternatives
- **Global competition**: Lower-cost production in other regions
- **Consumer price sensitivity**: Demand for cheaper fabrics

**Market changes**:
- **Fashion trends**: Casual dress, easier care fabrics
- **Care requirements**: Woolens require careful maintenance
- **Formal wear decline**: Less demand for traditional formal fabrics
- **Performance fabrics**: Synthetics often superior performance

**Industrial scale**:
- **Large-scale production**: Favored standardization and simplicity
- **Cost reduction**: Pressured elimination of complex, costly processes
- **Speed**: Faster production times favored
- **Consistency**: Industrial scale favored uniform, simple processes

### Technological Lock-out

**Synthetic fiber characteristics**:
- **No felting**: Synthetics don't felt like wool
- **Different surface structures**: Can't be napped traditionally
- **Engineered surfaces**: Surface characteristics built into fiber
- **Performance advantages**: Often superior in specific applications

**Manufacturing optimization**:
- **Fiber engineering**: Fibers designed for specific end uses
- **Integrated processing**: Fibers designed for optimal manufacturing
- **End-to-end optimization**: Not dependent on traditional finishing
- **New paradigms**: Rather than improving old methods

**Cumulative disadvantage**:
- Multiple factors aligned against traditional methods
- No single fatal flaw
- But combination of factors made traditional processes obsolete
- Alternatives cheaper, faster, more consistent, more versatile
